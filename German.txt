<p id="mwDg"><b id="mwDw">German</b> (<i id="mwEA">Deutsch</i>) is a Germanic language spoken by over 100 million people worldwide. It is the official and main language of <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Germany" title="Germany" id="mwEQ">Germany</a>, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Austria" title="Austria" id="mwEg">Austria</a>, and <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Liechtenstein" title="Liechtenstein" id="mwEw">Liechtenstein</a>. It is also an official language of <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Switzerland" title="Switzerland" id="mwFA">Switzerland</a>, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Luxembourg" title="Luxembourg" id="mwFQ">Luxembourg</a> and <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Belgium" title="Belgium" id="mwFg">Belgium</a>, and a national language in <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Namibia" title="Namibia" id="mwFw">Namibia</a>. German is also spoken by minorities in the French regions of <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Alsace" title="Alsace" id="mwGA">Alsace</a> and <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Lorraine" title="Lorraine" id="mwGQ">Lorraine</a>, in the northern Italian province of <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/South_Tyrol" title="South Tyrol" id="mwGg">South Tyrol</a>, and in a small part of southern <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Denmark" title="Denmark" id="mwGw">Denmark</a>. </p>

<p id="mwHA">Standard German (<i id="mwHQ">Hochdeutsch</i>, or High German) is also generally spoken by many as a second language in much of <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Central_Europe" title="Central Europe" id="mwHg">Central Europe</a>, with small groups of native speakers as well due to the historical influence of Austria (the former <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Empire" title="Austro-Hungarian Empire" id="mwHw">Austro-Hungarian Empire</a>) and Germany over the region. Small isolated communities can be found elsewhere in the world, though their language may be far from standard German and interspersed with loanwords from other languages.</p>

<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" id="mwIA" data-mw='{"autoGenerated":true}'/></section><section data-mw-section-id="1" id="mwIQ" aria-labelledby="Dialects"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Dialects">Dialects</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=German_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Dialects"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb" id="mwIg"><a href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/File:Continental_West_Germanic_languages.png" class="mw-file-description" id="mwIw"><img resource="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/File:Continental_West_Germanic_languages.png" src="//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/Continental_West_Germanic_languages.png/330px-Continental_West_Germanic_languages.png?utm_source=en.wikivoyage.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail" decoding="async" loading="lazy" srcset="//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/Continental_West_Germanic_languages.png/960px-Continental_West_Germanic_languages.png?utm_source=en.wikivoyage.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="1796" data-file-height="2332" data-file-type="bitmap" height="357" width="275" class="mw-file-element" id="mwJA"/></a><figcaption id="mwJQ">A rough division of the dialects of the German language.</figcaption></figure>
<p id="mwJg">There are very strong accentual and dialectic differences in German-speaking countries.  </p>

<p id="mwJw">Despite forty years of East–West partition, virtually all important distinguishing marks between dialects scale from North to South rather than from East to West and isoglosses (lines separating different ways of saying the same word) are almost always aligned with parallels rather than meridians.  </p>

<p id="mwKA">A German from the north and one from the south of the country can have great difficulty understanding each other's dialects. A particularly striking mark of standard German and Southern dialects is the "High German consonant shift" that marks High German separate from all other Germanic languages, giving rise to words like "Apfel" (Appel in Low German, apple in English) "Pfirsich" (peach) "Kirche" (church), "machen" (maken in Low German, make in English) or "Kind" (child, pronounced with a hint of "ch" between the K and the i in the extreme South) that sound similar in Low German and all other Germanic languages but different in High German. Standard German, or <b id="mwKQ">Hochdeutsch</b>, is universally known and taught, and although not everyone speaks it well, it can be understood by most German speakers.  </p>

<p id="mwKg">"Broad" dialect has faced a lot of stigma in much of Germany throughout the twentieth century and with increasing media exposure, social and geographic mobility, many people haven't been taught any nonstandard dialect by their parents and hardly picked anything up from their peers. Slightly "colored" German however is still common and the expert will be able to pinpoint the region of origin even though communication isn't usually hindered by these aspects. Generally, the further south one travels, the broader the influence of dialect on standard speech, with the Main River as a rough "border" between the northern and southern German-speaking cultural worlds. In Switzerland a (slightly adjusted for local needs) standard form of High German was kept, but the local dialects have retained so much ground - even the evening news might conduct an interview in Swiss German - that some Swiss fear a loss of <i id="mwKw">standard</i> German proficiency, not the "death of dialect" bemoaned in Germany. Austria is in a middle position with a slightly divergent standard and dialectal usage that is closer to the standard than in Switzerland but further from it than in Germany. </p>

<p id="mwLA">As a rule, one should not expect everyone (especially in the rural areas) in Alsace, Baden-Württemberg, Bavaria, Austria, South Tyrol and Switzerland to speak standard German well, but a dialect instead. In Alsace most people prefer to speak French with outsiders, and do <b id="mwLQ">not</b> consider their dialect to be German as such! Dialect, where still spoken, is very much for family and home. People in Alsace are often reluctant to speak High German with Germans! They are often less put off speaking their dialect to someone from Switzerland or Baden, due to the fact they are pretty much mutually intelligible.</p>

<p id="mwLg">In the north of Germany, some people speak a related Germanic language called <span lang="de" about="#mwt5" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwLw" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"de"},"2":{"wt":"Plattdüütsch"}},"i":0}}]}'>Plattdüütsch</span> or <b id="mwMA">Low German</b> (<span lang="de" about="#mwt6" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwMQ" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"de"},"2":{"wt":"Plattdeutsch"}},"i":0}}]}'>Plattdeutsch</span> in German). It is very similar to <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Dutch_phrasebook" title="Dutch phrasebook" id="mwMg">Dutch</a>. Nearly all Platt speakers also speak German.</p>

<p id="mwMw">The German spoken in <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Switzerland" title="Switzerland" id="mwNA">Switzerland</a> is referred to as <span lang="de" about="#mwt7" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwNQ" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"de"},"2":{"wt":"Schwiizertüütsch"}},"i":0}}]}'>Schwiizertüütsch</span> (<b id="mwNg"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Swiss_German_phrasebook" title="Swiss German phrasebook" class="mw-redirect" id="mwNw">Swiss German</a></b>). There are various varieties of Swiss German depending on the region, and it is even widely used in the media, though news broadcasts are in standard German. Dialects are not usually used in the media in Germany, Austria or Liechtenstein except for regional programming. Thus, this is rare in the German speaking world, as "Hochdeutsch" is more or less the sole language of media outside Switzerland. Nevertheless, all German-speaking Swiss learn standard German in school, so unless you approach somebody really old who has never been to school, you'll be fine. The German dialects spoken in Vorarlberg (Austria), Baden-Württemberg (Germany) and Alsace (France) are similar to Swiss German.</p>

<p id="mwOA"><b id="mwOQ">Swiss Standard German</b> (<span lang="de" about="#mwt8" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwOg" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"de"},"2":{"wt":"Schweizer Hochdeutsch"}},"i":0}}]}'>Schweizer Hochdeutsch</span>) is not the same as Swiss German, but a variant of standard German used for writing and formal speech in Switzerland. It has a few spelling and vocabulary quirks, but is otherwise only recognizable as distinct from Austrian or German standard German to the trained eye. The letter <span lang="de" about="#mwt9" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwOw" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"de"},"2":{"wt":"&apos;&apos;ß&apos;&apos;"}},"i":0}}]}'><i>ß</i></span> is absent in both Swiss German and Swiss Standard German; being replaced with "ss". And for example, <span lang="de" about="#mwt10" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwPA" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"de"},"2":{"wt":"&apos;&apos;Velo, Grüezi, parkieren&apos;&apos;"}},"i":0}}]}'><i>Velo, Grüezi, parkieren</i></span> is not used in Germany; they will be <span lang="de" about="#mwt11" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwPQ" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"de"},"2":{"wt":"&apos;&apos;Fahrrad, hallo, parken&apos;&apos;"}},"i":0}}]}'><i>Fahrrad, hallo, parken</i></span>.</p>

<p id="mwPg">Formal German in what was then East Germany also diverged in some points from West German, though never to the extent that Korean between the two Koreas diverged (North Korea and South Korea even use different terms for "Korea" in their official long form names) and many of the "East German" words fell out of use after reunification due to the thing they describe not existing or having been replaced by the Western counterpart. While dialects, particularly Saxon and Berlin-Brandenburgish still color East German speech, the GDR coinings are now mostly unused and unknown to the younger generation. These words are always labelled <i id="mwPw">DDR</i> or <i id="mwQA">historisch</i> in dictionaries. </p>

<p id="mwQQ">In the Italian South Tyrol, like in most of Austria, Switzerland, Liechtenstein and southern Germany, most people speak a local dialect. However, standard German and Italian are both taught in the schools. The German spoken in South Tyrol is very similar to that of neighboring Austria and Bavaria to the north.</p>

<p id="mwQg">Outside Europe, a dialect of German known as <b id="mwQw">Pennsylvania Dutch</b> is spoken by the Amish community in the <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/United_States_of_America" title="United States of America" class="mw-redirect" id="mwRA">United States</a>. A dialect of German is also spoken by the ethnic German community in <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Namibia" title="Namibia" id="mwRQ">Namibia</a>.</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id="2" id="mwRg" aria-labelledby="Pronunciation_guide"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Pronunciation_guide">Pronunciation guide</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=German_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Pronunciation guide"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwRw">German pronunciation is relatively straightforward, although spelling is somewhat more involved. </p>

<p id="mwSA"><i id="mwSQ">Y</i> and <i id="mwSg">qu</i> are uncommon in standard German, with close to 90% of the words using them being borrowed.</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id="4" id="mwgA" aria-labelledby="Length_of_vowels"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Length_of_vowels">Length of vowels</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=German_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Length of vowels"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwgQ">A vowel is shortened when followed by a double consonant letters. A vowel is lengthened by a subsequent 'h', or by a double vowel, depending on the word. An exception is 'i', which is lengthened by a following 'e' or 'eh'.</p>

<p id="mwgg">Examples: the <i id="mwgw">h</i> in <i id="mwhA">Hahn</i> makes the <i id="mwhQ">a</i> long; the <i id="mwhg">aa</i> in <i id="mwhw">Haar</i> is also long, the <i id="mwiA">e</i> in <i id="mwiQ">Tier</i> makes the <i id="mwig">i</i> long. (See below for "Diphthongs".)</p>

<p id="mwiw">In the Austrian and Bavarian dialect, vowel pronunciations are slightly longer and more pronounced.</p>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id="7" id="mwASQ" aria-labelledby="Grammar"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Grammar">Grammar</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=German_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Grammar"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwASU">Many languages in northern Europe belong to the Germanic language family, although German grammar itself retains many conjugations and declensions from proto-Germanic that have since been lost by other language relatives such as English. This also means that speakers of another conservative Germanic language like <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Icelandic_phrasebook" title="Icelandic phrasebook" id="mwASY">Icelandic</a> will find many aspects of German grammar familiar.</p>

<p id="mwAWQ">In common with many other European languages, German has two "you" verb forms which denote the relationship the speaker has to someone else. To express familiarity, one uses the <i id="mwAWU">du</i> form; for formality, the <i id="mwAWY">Sie</i> form. As a general rule the <i id="mwAWc">Sie</i> form is used when one might address someone as "Madam" or "Sir". If on first name terms, one uses the <i id="mwAWg">du</i> form. However, last name and "du" and first name and "Sie" are not entirely unheard of, especially in the context of certain professions. Unless the person you talk to allows you to use "du" (<i id="mwAWk">duzen</i>), use "Sie" - Germans as a whole are more cautious with friendly terms of address than - say - Americans and some people still address each other with "Sie" despite 20 years or more of working in the same office.</p>

<p id="mwAWo">German nouns are divided into 3 different genders: masculine, feminine and neuter. The article of a noun depends on the gender: <i id="mwAWs">der</i> (m),  <i id="mwAWw">die</i> (f) and <i id="mwAW0">das</i> (n). Unlike in English, inanimate objects frequently have, often arbitrarily, genders assigned to them; for example, <i id="mwAW4">Tisch</i> (table) is male, <i id="mwAW8">Tür</i> (door) is female, while <i id="mwAXA">Tor</i> (gate) is neuter. While the gender of nouns denoting a person usually correspond to their natural gender (for example <i id="mwAXE">Mutter</i> (mother) is female and <i id="mwAXI">Vater</i> (father) is male), there are some exceptions. A grammatical rule that overrides this includes the diminutive <i id="mwAXM">-chen</i> noun ending which will result in a neuter. For example, <i id="mwAXQ">Mädchen</i> (girl) is actually neuter, and not female as you'd expect. </p>

<p id="mwAXU">Third-person pronouns also depend on the grammatical gender of the subject: <i id="mwAXY">er</i>  (m), <i id="mwAXc">sie</i> (f) and <i id="mwAXg">es</i> (n). However, you will generally be understood if you use the wrong gender as there are only a few (obscure) nouns which mean different things depending on gender, and their correct meaning will always be clear from the context.</p>

<p id="mwAaQ">Furthermore, German nouns are declined. There are four grammatical cases: nominative (subject), accusative (direct object), genitive (possessive), and dative (indirect object). Each varies depending on the noun's gender and whether it is singular or plural.</p>

<p id="mwAaU">In an example, </p>
<dl id="mwAaY"><dd id="mwAac"><b id="mwAag">Ich gebe dem Mann den Apfel der Frau.</b></dd>
<dd id="mwAak">"I give the man the woman's apple."</dd></dl>
<p id="mwAao">The <b id="mwAas">Dativ</b> article is invoked in the noun <i id="mwAaw">Mann</i> to signify <b id="mwAa0">to whom</b> I give the apple, while the <b id="mwAa4">Akkusativ</b> article is invoked in the noun <i id="mwAa8">Apfel</i> to signify <b id="mwAbA">what</b> I am giving, and the <b id="mwAbE">Genitiv</b> article is invoked in the noun <i id="mwAbI">Frau</i> to signify <b id="mwAbM">whose</b> apple I give.</p>

<p id="mwAbQ">In common speech, particularly in certain dialects, the cases - particularly the Genitiv - have a tendency to "disappear" or be rendered in ways that would be seen as "wrong" from the standpoint of prescriptivist linguistics. A particularly common phenomenon is replacing the Genitiv with Dativ and "sein" (in this case a possessive meaning "his" or "its") or "ihr" (a possessive meaning "her"). A famous work on the (supposed or real) decline of the German language has thus been titled "Der Dativ ist dem Genitiv sein Tod" -  which could be rendered "The dative is the genitive his death" in English.</p>

<p id="mwAbU">All nouns, alongside the word Sie for you, always begin with a capital letter, even in the middle of a sentence. This is an important way to distinguish between some verbs and objects. It also arguably makes reading easier, though writing is somewhat complicated by the need to find out whether a verb or adjective is used in a substantivized form.</p>

<p id="mwAbY">Statement sentences generally follow the subject-verb-object structure alongside many other rules which are similar to English. Present tense and present continuous however, are not differentiated by default; one must add the word <i id="mwAbc">gerade</i> or <i id="mwAbg">jetzt</i> to specifically indicate that the action is happening now.</p>

<dl id="mwAbk"><dd id="mwAbo"><b id="mwAbs">Ich esse (nicht) den roten Apfel</b></dd>
<dd id="mwAbw">"I am (not) eating the red apple."</dd></dl>

<p id="mwAb0">In question sentences, the structure would typically be (question word)-verb-subject-noun.</p>
<dl id="mwAb4"><dd id="mwAb8"><b id="mwAcA">Was essen Sie?</b></dd>
<dd id="mwAcE">What are you eating?</dd></dl>

<dl id="mwAcI"><dd id="mwAcM"><b id="mwAcQ">Essen Sie den roten Apfel?</b></dd>
<dd id="mwAcU">Are you eating the red apple?</dd></dl>

<section data-mw-section-id="8" id="mwAcY" aria-labelledby="Addressing_people"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Addressing_people">Addressing people</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=German_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Addressing people"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwAcc">By default, addressing adult strangers and superiors requires <i id="mwAcg">Sie</i>, unless they explicitly use <i id="mwAck">du</i> when talking to you. The latter is commonly reserved for close friends, children and family members, and people of younger age.  Always use <i id="mwAco">Sie</i> when addressing police officers; the informal address may be taken as insulting otherwise, and the fine for insulting an officer is swingeing. </p>

<dl id="mwAcs"><dt id="mwAcw">Herr (pl., Herren)</dt><dd id="mwAc0">for men (equivalent to Mister in English). This word also means "master, owner, ruler, gentleman, sir" and is also a form of address for the Christian God (English equivalent: Lord).</dd>
<dt id="mwAc4">Frau (pl., Frauen)</dt><dd id="mwAc8">for women (equivalent to Ms. and Mrs. in English). This word means "woman" and is used in everyday speech for "wife".</dd>
<dt id="mwAdA">Dame (pl. Damen) (<i id="mwAdE">DAH-me</i>, NOT <i id="mwAdI">deim</i>)</dt><dd id="mwAdM">the formal German word for a woman. The salutation "Ladies and Gentlemen" would be translated to "<i id="mwAdQ">meine Damen und Herren</i>".</dd></dl>

<p id="mwAdU">The term <i id="mwAdY">Fräulein</i>, which literally means Miss in English, is now deprecated and even considered condescending.</p>

<p id="mwAdc">"Frau" (woman) and "Mann" (man) are used in everyday speech to refer to spouses.  Typically, the difference between "woman" or "wife" (or man vs husband) is signalled in the pronouns:  "eine Frau" refers to a woman generically ("a woman just bought the last ticket"), but "seine Frau" (his wife) or "meine Frau" (my wife) refers to the relationship.  If you need to emphasize the legal martial status, then use <i id="mwAdg">Ehefrau</i> (wife; literally "married woman") and <i id="mwAdk">Ehemann</i> (husband, literally "married man").  These are not forms of address, but ordinary nouns.  </p>

<p id="mwAdo">As Germans are particularly fond of their academic degrees, a Mr. Schmidt with a doctoral degree or as a doctor would be called <i id="mwAds">Herr Doktor Schmidt</i>. While this usage is more common in writing a letter than speaking, it is expected in a meeting with someone of a superior position, if they introduce themselves or are introduced with their full titles.</p>

<p id="mwAdw">Combining all of the above into an example, when Angela Merkel was chancellor, her husband could have introduce her as "meine Ehefrau, Frau Bundeskanzlerin Doktorin Angela Merkel" ("my wife, Ms. Chancellor Dr. Angela Merkel"), and she could introduce him as "meine Ehemann, Herr Professor Doktor Joachim Sauer" (my husband, Mr. Prof. Dr. Joachim Sauer).</p>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id="9" id="mwAd0" aria-labelledby="Cognates_in_English"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Cognates_in_English">Cognates in English</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=German_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Cognates in English"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwAd4">Due to their shared Germanic roots and common borrowings from Latin, Greek and French, English speakers will find many German words easy to understand (for example, <i id="mwAd8">Glas</i> means "glass"; <i id="mwAeA">gut</i> means "good"; <i id="mwAeE">kommen</i> means "come"; <i id="mwAeI">hier</i> means "here", <i id="mwAeM">Mann</i> means "man" or "husband"; <i id="mwAeQ">Schuhe</i> are "shoes"; <i id="mwAeU">Finger</i> is "finger"; <i id="mwAeY">Schule</i> is "school", <i id="mwAec">Hund</i> is "dog" [cognate with "hound"]), and some are even identical, such as <i id="mwAeg">Hotel</i>, <i id="mwAek">Baby</i> and <i id="mwAeo">Job</i> (except that they are capitalized, as are all nouns in German), and also <i id="mwAes">Restaurant</i>, though it's pronounced as in French, with a nasal "N" and no "T" sound at the end; and <i id="mwAew">Emotion</i>, which is pronounced "eh-mo-TSYON".</p>

<p id="mwAe0">In addition, it is very useful to know that words that take the ending "-less" in English typically take "-los" in German and those that end with "-free" take "-frei", so that the words <i id="mwAe4">kostenlos</i> and <i id="mwAe8">kostenfrei</i>, which literally translate as "costless" and "cost-free", both mean free (of charge). Similarly, words ending in "-ful" typically end in "-voll" in German, so that <i id="mwAfA">wundervoll</i> means "wonderful". Moreover, the comparative ending "-er" is used at least as much in German as in English. For example, <i id="mwAfE">fetter</i> is "fatter", <i id="mwAfI">lauter</i> is "louder", <i id="mwAfM">kälter</i> is "colder" and <i id="mwAfQ">besser</i> is "better".</p>

<p id="mwAfU">However, beware that there are many false friends, and quite a few are very common words. For example <i id="mwAfY">bekommen</i> means "get" ("become" is <i id="mwAfc">werden</i>), <i id="mwAfg">wer</i> means "who" ("where" is <i id="mwAfk">wo</i>), <i id="mwAfo">denn</i> means "since" ("then" is <i id="mwAfs">dann</i>), <i id="mwAfw">weil</i> means "because" (not while), <i id="mwAf0">vor</i> means "before" ("for" is <i id="mwAf4">für</i>), <i id="mwAf8">übersehen</i> actually means "overlook", in the sense of failing to consider something (the German for "oversee" in the usual sense of "supervise" is <i id="mwAgA">überwachen</i>) and <i id="mwAgE">Flur</i> means "corridor" ("floor" in the sense of "storey" is <i id="mwAgI">Etage</i>, and in the sense of the surface you walk on is <i id="mwAgM">Boden</i> or <i id="mwAgQ">Fußboden</i>). Be careful about the usage of the word <i id="mwAgU">komisch</i>, which sounds like it should mean "comical" but usually has a negative connotation of "weird", instead. <i id="mwAgY">Frei</i> very commonly means "available", rather than free of charge. Also, <i id="mwAgc">Eis</i> usually means ice cream, so if you ask for <i id="mwAgg">ein Glas Kaffee mit Eis</i>, you may get coffee with ice cream, not iced coffee. <i id="mwAgk">Kalter Kaffee</i> ("cold coffee") should be a safe request if you want iced coffee. Even when a word looks identical, the meaning can be completely different, as with <i id="mwAgo">Gift</i>, which means "poison", and <i id="mwAgs">Handy</i>, which means "mobile phone"!</p>

<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb" id="mwAxw"><a href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/File:Euro_coins_and_banknotes.jpg" class="mw-file-description" id="mwAx0"><img resource="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/File:Euro_coins_and_banknotes.jpg" src="//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/65/Euro_coins_and_banknotes.jpg/250px-Euro_coins_and_banknotes.jpg?utm_source=en.wikivoyage.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail" decoding="async" loading="lazy" srcset="//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/65/Euro_coins_and_banknotes.jpg/500px-Euro_coins_and_banknotes.jpg?utm_source=en.wikivoyage.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="2272" data-file-height="1704" data-file-type="bitmap" height="188" width="250" class="mw-file-element" id="mwAx4"/></a><figcaption id="mwAx8">Know how much to pay when shopping.</figcaption></figure>
<p id="mwAyA">In German, the roles of dot and comma are swapped compared to their English counterparts.
The grouping separator in big numbers is a dot (.), not a comma(,); the separator between decimal fractions and integer is a comma (,), not a dot (.).</p>

<p id="mwAyE">Numbers above twenty are said "backwards". Twenty-one (einundzwanzig) is literally spoken as "one-and-twenty". This takes a bit of getting used to, especially in higher regions. Eg. 53,426 (dreiundfünfzigtausendvierhundertsechsundzwanzig) is spoken as "three-and-fifty-thousand-four-hundred-six-and-twenty". Native English speakers may note that the nursery rhyme 'Four and twenty blackbirds' as well as some older literature (Sherlock Holmes for example) uses this convention from medieval English. It's also helpful in learning the number system in the German language by listening to "Eins, Twei, Polizei" by Mo-Do.</p>

<p id="mwAyI">Unlike English, German uses the long scale for larger numbers, so <i id="mwAyM">eine Billion</i> and <i id="mwAyQ">eine Trillion</i> are not the same as the English "one billion" and "one trillion".</p>

<div style="font-weight:bold;">Telling time</div>

<p>While many Germans use the 24-hour format for times, they often use 12-hour times in conversations. There is not much use of "am" or "pm", although you can add "<i>vormittags</i>" (before noon) and "<i>nachmittags</i>" (after noon) when it's not clear from the context. A significant difference is the convention for 'half past', when at 07:30 English speakers would say "half (past) seven", whereas Germans say "<i>halb acht</i>" ("half {to} eight"). How to express 07:15 or 07:45 is something of a shibboleth for several dialects and even some Germans might not understand the form they did not grow up with. One way to say it is following English logic à la "quarter past x" making 07:15 come out as "viertel nach sieben" and 07:45 as "viertel vor acht". In other areas the hour is told according to the portion of the way to the next hour: "viertel acht" (literally quarter eight) means 07:15, "halb acht" means 07:30 and "dreiviertel acht" means 07:45. People who use the latter system will usually know (but dislike) the former. People who use the former system tend to draw blanks when confronted with the latter.  However, it is <i>always</i> "halb acht" and <i>never</i> "halb nach sieben".</p>

<p>If in doubt, use the twenty-hour clock.</p></td></tr>

<section data-mw-section-id="17" id="mwA_g" aria-labelledby="Clock_time"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading4"><h4 id="Clock_time">Clock time</h4><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=German_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=17" title="Edit section: Clock time"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb" id="mwA_k"><a href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/File:BahnhofsuhrZuerich_RZ.jpg" class="mw-file-description" id="mwA_o"><img resource="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/File:BahnhofsuhrZuerich_RZ.jpg" src="//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b7/BahnhofsuhrZuerich_RZ.jpg/250px-BahnhofsuhrZuerich_RZ.jpg?utm_source=en.wikivoyage.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail" decoding="async" loading="lazy" srcset="//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b7/BahnhofsuhrZuerich_RZ.jpg/500px-BahnhofsuhrZuerich_RZ.jpg?utm_source=en.wikivoyage.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="1100" data-file-height="1100" data-file-type="bitmap" height="250" width="250" class="mw-file-element" id="mwA_s"/></a><figcaption id="mwA_w">Punctuality is of paramount importance in German-speaking countries!</figcaption></figure>
<p id="mwA_0">In German speaking countries as in many other European countries, it's usual to use a 24 hour clock, ranging from 0.00 to 24.00. Okay, 24.00 is actually the same as 0.00, but one day later. In conversation however, a 12-hour format is also commonly used, as long as one understands the context of which time of day it is.</p>

<dl id="mwA_4"><dt id="mwA_8">one o'clock AM (01<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBAA">:</span>00)</dt><dd id="mwBAE">ein Uhr (<i id="mwBAI">IGHN oor</i>)</dd>
<dt id="mwBAM">two o'clock AM (02<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBAQ">:</span>00)</dt><dd id="mwBAU">zwei Uhr (<i id="mwBAY">TSVIGH oor</i>)</dd>
<dt id="mwBAc">noon (12<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBAg">:</span>00)</dt><dd id="mwBAk">zwölf Uhr <i id="mwBAo">or</i> Mittag (<i id="mwBAs">TSVOOLF oor</i> or <i id="mwBAw">MIT-tahk</i>)</dd>
<dt id="mwBA0">one o'clock PM (13<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBA4">:</span>00)</dt><dd id="mwBA8">dreizehn Uhr (<i id="mwBBA">DRIGH-tsayn oor</i>)</dd>
<dt id="mwBBE">two o'clock PM (14<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBBI">:</span>00)</dt><dd id="mwBBM">vierzehn Uhr (<i id="mwBBQ">FEER-tsayn oor</i>)</dd>
<dt id="mwBBU">midnight (00<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBBY">:</span>00 or 24<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBBc">:</span>00)</dt><dd id="mwBBg">Mitternacht <i id="mwBBk">or</i> null Uhr <i id="mwBBo">or</i> vierundzwanzig Uhr (<i id="mwBBs">MIT-er-nahkht</i> or <i id="mwBBw">NOOL oor</i> or <i id="mwBB0">FEER-oont-TSVAHN-tsikh oor</i>)</dd></dl>

<p id="mwBB4">Mentioning the time with the hour and minute is not unlike the English convention. See exceptions below.</p>
<dl id="mwBB8"><dt id="mwBCA">eight oh one AM or one past eight AM (08<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBCE">:</span>01)</dt><dd id="mwBCI">acht Uhr eins (<i id="mwBCM">AKHT oor IGHNS</i>) or eins nach acht (<i id="mwBCQ">IGHNS nakh AKHT</i>)</dd>
<dt id="mwBCU">Seven fifty nine PM or one to eight PM (19<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBCY">:</span>59)</dt><dd id="mwBCc">neunzehn Uhr neunundfünfzig (<i id="mwBCg">noin-ZEEN oor Fünf-und-noin-TSIG</i>) or eins vor acht (<i id="mwBCk">IGHNS for TSVAN-tsig</i>) ("eins vor zwanzig" sounds unidiomatic)</dd></dl>

<p id="mwBCo">Expressing "fractional hours" differs slightly among various regions. The "normal" way of doing it is: <br id="mwBCs"/></p>

<ul id="mwBCw"><li id="mwBC0">Quarter past one (01<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBC4">:</span>15) - <i id="mwBC8">Viertel nach eins</i> or <i id="mwBDA">Viertel zwei</i></li>
<li id="mwBDE">Half past one (01<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBDI">:</span>30) - <i id="mwBDM">Halb zwei</i> (half two)</li>
<li id="mwBDQ">A quarter to two (01<span typeof="mw:Nowiki" id="mwBDU">:</span>45) - <i id="mwBDY">Viertel vor zwei</i> or <i id="mwBDc">Dreiviertel zwei</i></li></ul>

<p id="mwBDg">The latter form is common in Eastern Germany, Bavaria, and Austria, although the former is universally understood but not without causing cringes. Outside these regions, many have trouble understanding the latter form. Usually Germans who don't understand you will ask and saying the number (11:45 "elf Uhr fünfundvierzig") is sure to get the confusion out of the way, though it may sound somewhat stilted and bureaucratic.</p>

<p id="mwBDk">To ask for time:</p>
<dl id="mwBDo"><dt id="mwBDs">What time is it?</dt>
<dd id="mwBDw">Wie spät ist es? (<i id="mwBD0">Wee SPAET ist es?</i>) <br id="mwBD4"/>Wie viel Uhr ist es? (<i id="mwBD8">Wee VEEL Ur ist es?</i>)</dd></dl>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id="21" id="mwBLs" aria-labelledby="Time_and_date_format"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Time_and_date_format">Time and date format</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=German_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=21" title="Edit section: Time and date format"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwBLw">In the clock time, hours and minutes are separated by a '.' instead of ':', though the latter is also widely used. Another way is to write the minutes raised like an exponent.</p>

<p id="mwBL0">The date is always written in the order day, month, year, e.g.:
12/10/2003 is in German 10.12.2003.
10th of December 2003 is in German 10. Dezember 2003. </p>

<p id="mwBL4">Years prior to 2000 are pronounced like this example: 1957 neunzehn-hundert-sieben-und-fünfzig (the - are only here for clarity, it would be written as one word, when written down, a literal translation would be nineteen-hundred-seven-and-fifty) So far years after 2000 are pronounced like this example: 2014 zwei-tausend-vierzehn (again, written as one word, a literal translation would be two-thousand-fourteen)</p>

<p id="mwBL8">Dates are always read like an ordinal number. When it stands alone, add the suffix -r at the ordinal number; Germans will also often mention the months in numbers (i.e. 1st of January= erster erster or der erste erste). When the date is used as part of a sentence (e.g. We fly on the 1st of May), a <i id="mwBMA">dativ</i> case is invoked, in which you must add the suffix -n after the ordinal number (i.e. Wir fliegen am ersten Mai).</p>

<div style="font-weight:bold;">How to build a German noun</div>

<p>In a similar way as English, compound words that make a noun are also common in German. The difference however is that all these words are stacked into a single word (<b>agglutinative</b>). While initially anyone reading the word is guaranteed to freak out, breaking them one by one would then make sense. While only few words are useful for travelers and even for Germans themselves, cardinal numbers are the most commonly used examples. (e.g. 678429<span typeof="mw:DisplaySpace"> </span>: <i>sechshundertachtundsiebzigtausendvierhundertneunundzwanzig</i>).</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id="33" id="mwCFc" aria-labelledby="Country_names"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Country_names">Country names</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=German_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=33" title="Edit section: Country names"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwCFg">Names of countries in general retain their official name or equivalent to English words, with subtle adaptations suitable for German speakers. Some country names which end with -ia (Latin suffix) are either adapted into -en (e.g.: Egypt to Ägypten, India to Indien, Romania to Rumänien) or retained (e.g. Malaysia, Nigeria, Panama). Some countries also take a <i id="mwCFk">definite article</i> of either "der" (e.g. der Irak, der Iran, der Libanon) or "die" in singular (e.g. die Schweiz, die Ukraine, die Türkei, die Mongolei, die Slowakei, all countries ending with -ei, countries containing the name Republik) or plural form (e.g. die Niederlande, die USA, die VAE (the UAE), all countries of plural form in English).</p>

<p id="mwCFo">Countries with significant spelling and pronunciation differences compared to English are listed below. </p>

<p id="mwCFs">In general there has been a tendency since about the 1950s to move away from "Germanized" pronunciations and spellings towards more Anglophone or more akin to the local name ones. A somewhat dicey subject are German names for formerly German places (e.g. <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Wroclaw" title="Wroclaw" class="mw-redirect" id="mwCFw">Wroclaw</a>) which will be understood but might be seen as a revanchist statement by some. Similarly in some country names the c used to be replaced with a k but isn't any more (e.g. "Nikaragua" is hardly used any more) whereas for "Mexiko" and "Kolumbien" the k form is standard.</p>

<p id="mwCF0">For indicating the nationality of a person, add the suffix -er at the end or replacing the -en suffix if the country has the suffix. Leave it as it is for male or add -in for female.</p>

<p id="mwCF4">In the following list, there are articles like,</p>

<p id="mwCF8"><i id="mwCGA">die</i> (<i id="mwCGE">dee</i>): feminine or plural
<br id="mwCGI"/>
<i id="mwCGM">der</i> (<i id="mwCGQ">deer</i>): masculine </p>

<p id="mwCGU">In German grammar, <i id="mwCGY">das</i> is rarely used in countries' names. The grammar needs an article before adjective with the name, e.g. <i id="mwCGc">das große Spanien</i> ('the big Spain').</p>
