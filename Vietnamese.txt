<b id="mwBg">Vietnamese</b> (<i id="mwBw">Tiếng Việt</i>) is one of the most spoken languages in the world, with around 90 million native speakers. It is the official language in <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Vietnam" title="Vietnam" id="mwCA">Vietnam</a> and also spoken in places where Vietnamese have immigrated to such as the <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/United_States" title="United States" id="mwCQ">United States</a>, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/France" title="France" id="mwCg">France</a>, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Australia" title="Australia" id="mwCw">Australia</a> and <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Canada" title="Canada" id="mwDA">Canada</a>. It is also the native language of the <b id="mwDQ">Gin</b> or <b id="mwDg">Jing</b> ethnic group from some islands off the coast of <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Guangxi" title="Guangxi" id="mwDw">Guangxi</a> province in southern <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/China" title="China" id="mwEA">China</a>, who are ethnically and culturally Vietnamese but citizens of China.</p>

<p id="mwEQ">Vietnamese is part of the <b id="mwEg">Mon-Khmer language family</b>. It is related to the <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Khmer_phrasebook" title="Khmer phrasebook" id="mwEw">Khmer</a> language spoken in neighbouring Cambodia, but the two languages are not mutually intelligible. Vietnamese was more influenced by <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Chinese_phrasebook" title="Chinese phrasebook" id="mwFA">Chinese</a>, while Khmer was more influenced by <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Sanskrit_phrasebook" title="Sanskrit phrasebook" id="mwFQ">Sanskrit</a>.</p>

<p id="mwFg">Vietnamese grammar is very simple: nouns and adjectives don't have genders, and verbs aren't conjugated. Vietnamese is a tonal language; the meaning of a word depends on how high or low your voice is. Vietnamese is not related to Chinese, but Classical Chinese was the literary language of Vietnam prior to French colonization, and the Vietnamese language contains many loan words from Chinese, even using Chinese-based characters as its writing system, called "chữ Nôm", until the French replaced it with the Latin alphabet. As a result of the French colonisation, modern Vietnamese also contains numerous loan words from <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/French_phrasebook" title="French phrasebook" id="mwFw">French</a>.</p>

<p id="mwGA">In modern times, Vietnamese is generally written in the Latin alphabet, with chữ Nôm mostly having fallen out of use, except for certain ceremonial and religious purposes.</p>

<p id="mwGQ">Vietnamese is traditionally divided into three different regional dialect groups: North, Central and South. The Northern dialect, as spoken in Hanoi, is the prestige dialect that is used in news broadcasts, and all Vietnamese pop singers are expected to sing in the Northern dialect regardless of what dialect they speak. That said, if you are based in Vietnam's main economic centre in the South (around Ho Chi Minh City), the Southern dialect is what you will hear in everyday life. The Southern dialect also tends to be more prevalent in overseas Vietnamese communities, due to the Southern origin of most of the refugees who fled in the aftermath of the Vietnam War.</p>

<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" id="mwGg" data-mw='{"autoGenerated":true}'/></section><section data-mw-section-id="1" id="mwGw" aria-labelledby="Pronunciation_guide"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Pronunciation_guide">Pronunciation guide</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Vietnamese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Pronunciation guide"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwHA">Vietnamese spelling is more or less phonetic, and generally similar to Portuguese (which it is based on). Once you figure out how to pronounce each letter and tone, you have a pretty good idea of how to pronounce Vietnamese, which has very few exceptions compared to English.</p>

<p id="mwHQ">Unless otherwise indicated, pronunciation throughout this phrasebook is for Southern (Saigon) Vietnamese, which is quite different from Northern (Hanoi), North Central (Vinh) or Central (Hue) Vietnamese.</p>

<section data-mw-section-id="2" id="mwHg" aria-labelledby="Vowels"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Vowels">Vowels</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Vietnamese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Vowels"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>

<dt id="mwMQ">e</dt>
<dd id="mwMg">like 'e' in "w<b id="mwMw">e</b>d": <i id="mwNA">tr<b id="mwNQ">e</b></i> (means "bamboo").</dd>

<dt id="mwWw">y</dt>
<dd id="mwXA">like 'ee' in "s<b id="mwXQ">ee</b>". Same pronunciation as "i".</dd></dl>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id="6" id="mw2A" aria-labelledby="Grammar"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Grammar">Grammar</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Vietnamese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Grammar"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mw2Q">One important difference between Vietnamese and Western languages is that Vietnamese has no polite equivalent of the second-person pronoun, "you." Only very close friends use the second-person pronoun "Mày" (pronounced "mhay" with a heavy A and accentuated Y), as it is considered very impolite between strangers. It is roughly equivalent to the pronoun "Omae" in Japanese. Consider it the extreme version of misusing "Toi" in place of "Vous" in French, except there is no equivalent of "Vous" in Vietnamese. Like with many other Asian cultures, it is more socially acceptable to be aware of your formal/informal relationship to another person, and imply it through the word you use to address them.</p>

<p id="mw2g">Strange as it might sound to Western ears, conversational Vietnamese avoids pronouns altogether and takes place almost entirely in the third person. For example, instead of saying "I think you are very beautiful" to a girl you like, you might say, "This older male thinks this younger female is very beautiful" or abridge it to "This younger female is very beautiful." There is always an overt implication in how you address someone according to their age and sex.</p>

<p id="mw2w">To Western ears, talking in the third person sounds stilted and pretentious, but to Vietnamese ears, it is the social norm. Vietnamese has a word for "I", <i id="mw3A">tôi</i>, but Vietnamese would use it only in abstract or formal situations (such as public speaking, addressing a television camera, or writing in a book.) Only foreigners use <i id="mw3Q">tôi</i> in conversation, which sounds stilted to Vietnamese, but they understand why it is done and come to expect it.</p>

<p id="mw3g">In conversational Vietnamese, the proper way to refer to yourself and others depends on a hierarchy of age and sex. Many of the terms  have a literal meaning of family relationships, though they are used for all people on all occasions. Options include:</p>
<ul id="mw3w"><li id="mw4A"><i id="mw4Q">Bạn</i> (friend, pronounced "bhang" with a heavy A. Easily confused with the word "table" to hilarious effect.)</li>
<li id="mw4g"><i id="mw4w">Con</i> (child, pronounced "kon", and <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw5A">–</span> parents will be amused <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw5Q">–</span> also means animal, for example "Con chim" literally means "(that) animal (which is a) bird".)</li>
<li id="mw5g"><i id="mw5w">Em</i> (literally, younger person, generally reserved for a younger sister, younger female relative, or a female acquaintance whom you consider equal to or younger than you <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw6A">–</span> refers to anyone younger than you but older than a child. It is the usual way to address your wife, girlfriend, or female lover, regardless of your own age or sex, with implications of endearment beyond daily usage of the word. Can be considered the equivalent of "my dear".)</li>
<li id="mw6Q"><i id="mw6g">Anh</i> (older brother <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw6w">–</span> man older than you by up to 10-20 years depending on how close they are. Or refers to a man of the same age as you, but whom you hold in high regard even if you are slightly older. It is also the usual way to address a husband, boyfriend, or male lover, regardless of your own age or sex, with implications of endearment beyond daily usage.)<br id="mw7A"/></li>
<li id="mw7Q"><i id="mw7g">Chị</i> (older sister <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw7w">–</span> woman older than you by up to 10-20 years depending on how close they are, with the implication that you feel the age between you and her does not matter. Generally only used for females slightly older than you.)</li>
<li id="mw8A"><i id="mw8Q">Chú</i> (literally, "Mister" with implications toward "uncle". Also used to address your father's younger brother <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw8g">–</span> man older than you and who you feel deserves the distinction beyond "Anh".)</li>
<li id="mw8w"><i id="mw9A">Cô</i> (literally, "Miss" or "Young Mrs." <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw9Q">–</span> woman older than you by 10+ years, or your female teacher prior to college. Implies that you feel she is a generation older than you, but you still think she is too young to be called "Madam" or "Mrs.")</li>
<li id="mw9g"><i id="mw9w">Bác</i> (unisex term, used for both Sir and Madam, <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw-A">–</span> refers to a mature person, generally 40 to 60 years old. Polite in that it implies you do not think the person is a senior or elderly yet.)</li>
<li id="mw-Q"><i id="mw-g">Ông</i> (literally, "old gentleman", grandfather <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw-w">–</span> refers specifically to a senior man, 50-60+ years old depending on how close you are.)</li>
<li id="mw_A"><i id="mw_Q">Bà</i> (literally, "Madam" or "elderly lady", grandmother <span typeof="mw:Entity" id="mw_g">–</span> refers specifically to a senior woman, 50-60+ years old depending on how close you are.)</li></ul>

<p id="mw_w">Choose one from the list to represent yourself, and one to represent the person you are talking to, depending on sex and relative age. For example, to get the attention of a waiter or waitress in a restaurant, say <i id="mwAQA">em/anh/chị/... ơi</i> (ơi being the ubiquitous Vietnamese term for "hey"). If you listen closely, when people address you or talk about you in Vietnamese, they will be using these terms. They will be very impressed if you can master this! Nonetheless, even between native Vietnamese speakers, it can get awkward when you try to figure out how to address someone who appears to be the same sex and, as far as you can tell, about the same age as you. Once you figure out their age and sex, they may have you use one of the above terms, or simply be amiable and ask you to call them "Bạn", or "friend".</p>

<p id="mwAQE">In formal situations, Vietnamese people generally refer to each other by a title and their second or third given name. Surnames are generally not used to address people in Vietnam. For instance, Hồ Chí Minh has the surname Hồ, but would ordinarily be address as Bác Minh in Vietnamese.</p>

<p id="mwAQI">For simplicity, however, many phrases below are translated without the relevant terms for you and/or your listener: For example, "How are you" is literally translated as "Healthy or not?" It is generally impolite to speak to a person without directly addressing them unless they're a subordinate, but Vietnamese usually don't take offense when foreigners omit this. Wherever you see <i id="mwAQM">tôi</i> below, you can substitute one of the words above according to the circumstance.</p>
