<b id=""mwBg"">Korean</b> (한국어 <i id=""mwBw"">hangugeo</i> or 한국말 <i id=""mwCA"">hangugmal</i> in South Korea, 조선어 <i id=""mwCQ"">chosŏnŏ</i> or 조선말 <i id=""mwCg"">chosŏnmal</i> in North Korea and China, 우리말 <i id=""mwCw"">urimal</i> (our language) or 국어 <i id=""mwDA"">gugeo</i> (national language) as a neutral denomination) is spoken in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/South_Korea"" title=""South Korea"" id=""mwDQ"">South</a> and <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/North_Korea"" title=""North Korea"" id=""mwDg"">North Korea</a>, as well as parts of <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Northeast_China"" title=""Northeast China"" id=""mwDw"">Northeast China</a> (where it is an official language in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Yanbian"" title=""Yanbian"" id=""mwEA"">Yanbian</a>). It may be distantly related to <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Japanese"" title=""Japanese"" class=""mw-redirect"" id=""mwEQ"">Japanese</a>, but is certainly entirely distinct from <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Chinese"" title=""Chinese"" class=""mw-redirect"" id=""mwEg"">Chinese</a>, although it uses large amounts of imported Chinese vocabulary.</p>

<p id=""mwEw"">Depending on which part of Korea you go to different dialects of Korean are spoken. The standard in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/South_Korea"" title=""South Korea"" id=""mwFA"">South Korea</a> is based on the Seoul dialect, which is spoken in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Seoul"" title=""Seoul"" id=""mwFQ"">Seoul</a> and <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Gyeonggi"" title=""Gyeonggi"" id=""mwFg"">Gyeonggi</a> province as well as the city of <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Kaesong"" title=""Kaesong"" id=""mwFw"">Kaesong</a> in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/North_Korea"" title=""North Korea"" id=""mwGA"">North Korea</a>, while the standard in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/North_Korea"" title=""North Korea"" id=""mwGQ"">North Korea</a> is based on the Pyongan dialect, which is spoken in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Pyongyang"" title=""Pyongyang"" id=""mwGg"">Pyongyang</a> as well as <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/North_Pyongan?action=edit&amp;redlink=1"" title=""North Pyongan (page does not exist)"" class=""new"" typeof=""mw:LocalizedAttrs"" id=""mwGw"" data-mw-i18n='{""title"":{""lang"":""x-page"",""key"":""red-link-title"",""params"":[""North Pyongan""]}}'>North</a> and <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/South_Pyongan?action=edit&amp;redlink=1"" title=""South Pyongan (page does not exist)"" class=""new"" typeof=""mw:LocalizedAttrs"" id=""mwHA"" data-mw-i18n='{""title"":{""lang"":""x-page"",""key"":""red-link-title"",""params"":[""South Pyongan""]}}'>South Pyongan</a> provinces. Other dialects include the Gyeongsang dialect spoken in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Busan"" title=""Busan"" id=""mwHQ"">Busan</a>, <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Daegu"" title=""Daegu"" id=""mwHg"">Daegu</a>, <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Ulsan"" title=""Ulsan"" id=""mwHw"">Ulsan</a> and the provinces of <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/North_Gyeongsang"" title=""North Gyeongsang"" id=""mwIA"">North</a> and <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/South_Gyeongsang"" title=""South Gyeongsang"" id=""mwIQ"">South Gyeongsang</a>, the Jeju dialect spoken on the island of <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Jeju"" title=""Jeju"" id=""mwIg"">Jeju</a>, and the Hamgyong dialect spoken in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/North_Hamgyong?action=edit&amp;redlink=1"" title=""North Hamgyong (page does not exist)"" class=""new"" typeof=""mw:LocalizedAttrs"" id=""mwIw"" data-mw-i18n='{""title"":{""lang"":""x-page"",""key"":""red-link-title"",""params"":[""North Hamgyong""]}}'>North</a> and <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/South_Hamgyong?action=edit&amp;redlink=1"" title=""South Hamgyong (page does not exist)"" class=""new"" typeof=""mw:LocalizedAttrs"" id=""mwJA"" data-mw-i18n='{""title"":{""lang"":""x-page"",""key"":""red-link-title"",""params"":[""South Hamgyong""]}}'>South Hamgyong</a> provinces. The dialects spoken by the ethnic Korean minority in China also differ by region, with those from <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Jilin"" title=""Jilin"" id=""mwJQ"">Jilin</a> mainly speaking the Hamgyong dialect, those from <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Liaoning"" title=""Liaoning"" id=""mwJg"">Liaoning</a> mainly speaking the Pyongan dialect, and those from <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Heilongjiang"" title=""Heilongjiang"" id=""mwJw"">Heilongjiang</a> mainly speaking the Gyeongsang dialect. This guide is based on the standard in South Korea.</p>

<p id=""mwKA"">The Korean language is fundamentally the same in North and South Korea, and speakers from both sides of the border are, for the most part, able to understand each other. The main differences lie in the loan words used to represent modern concepts, where South Koreans typically use English words, and North Koreans typically use the corresponding Russian words or create new words instead. The standard form of Korean used by the ethnic Korean minority in China mostly follows the North Korean standard, albeit with a significant number of loan words from Mandarin for modern concepts, though due to the popularity of South Korean dramas, most people also understand South Korean terms.</p>

<meta property=""mw:PageProp/toc"" id=""mwLg"" data-mw='{""autoGenerated"":true}'/></section><section data-mw-section-id=""1"" id=""mwLw"" aria-labelledby=""Grammar""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading2""><h2 id=""Grammar"">Grammar</h2><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Korean_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1"" title=""Edit section: Grammar""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwMA"">Korean sentence structure is very similar to that of <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Japanese_phrasebook"" title=""Japanese phrasebook"" id=""mwMQ"">Japanese</a>, so speakers of Japanese will find many aspects of Korean grammar familiar, and vice versa. But there are similar but slight differences to the standardized pronunciations, and the Korean language, even after its simplification in the past century, has a wider library of vowels and consonants than Japanese, hence Japanese speakers may find it difficult to pronounce various words, let alone transcribe them.</p>

<p id=""mwMg"">Korean word order is subject-object-verb: ""I-subject him-object see-verb."" Subjects (especially <i id=""mwMw"">I</i> and <i id=""mwNA"">you</i>) are often omitted if these are clear from the context. This may seem awkward from an English perspective, but English too has colloquial 1st-person/2nd-person subject omissions, such as ""[Are you] Done yet?"" or ""[I'm] Done."" It is a matter of whether sentences are common enough that such lack of subjects doesn't confuse the listener. In turn, some English colloquial sentences without subjects may be confusing from a Korean standpoint.</p>

<p id=""mwNQ"">There are no articles, genders, or declensions. It has extensive verb conjugations indicating tense and honorific level. There is a handy, universal plural form, but it is very often omitted.</p>

<p id=""mwNg"">Korean has postpositions instead of prepositions: <i id=""mwNw"">jip mite</i>, ""house below"" instead of ""below the house.""</p>

<p id=""mwOA"">Koreans refer to each other rather in terms like elder brother, elder sister, younger sibling, uncle, aunt, grandmother, grandfather, manager, teacher etc. (like Nepali or Chinese) than by using the word <i id=""mwOQ"">you</i>. Additionally, it's not uncommon to refer to yourself by using such an expression ( example: ""[I] Father will cook you a nice dinner."" Which feels like saying ""<i id=""mwOg"">This</i> father will...""). You can also call somebody an aunt, uncle or brother even if this person is actually not. Many Korean girls call even their boyfriend ""oppa"" (<i id=""mwOw"">older brother</i>).</p>

<p id=""mwPA"">Keep in mind that the words for older brother and older sister differ depending on the gender of the subject. The word for older brother is 형 (<i id=""mwPQ"">hyeong</i>) if the subject is male, and 오빠 (<i id=""mwPg"">oppa</i>) if the subject is female, while the word for older sister is 누나 (<i id=""mwPw"">nuna</i>) if the subject is male, and 언니 (<i id=""mwQA"">eonni</i>) if the subject is female.</p>

<p id=""mwQQ"">Depending on the relation to the person you have conversation with, it's necessary to find the correct level of formality and politeness. If the person is considered to be higher in the hierarchy, a very polite and formal form has to be used, while this person will use a more ""vernacular"" form to address you as a lower person. Koreans often ask very personal questions (about your age, occupation, family status etc.) in order to find out which form they should use when talking to you. This phrasebook assumes the highest formality level in most cases. Not only are words conjugated according to 6 existing levels of formality (but 2 are becoming unused), but a few words will also be replaced with different words altogether. Extremely formal places will often use some Chinese postal words as well.</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""2"" id=""mwQg"" aria-labelledby=""Pronunciation_guide""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading2""><h2 id=""Pronunciation_guide"">Pronunciation guide</h2><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Korean_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2"" title=""Edit section: Pronunciation guide""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwQw"">The good news is that unlike <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Chinese_phrasebook"" title=""Chinese phrasebook"" id=""mwRA"">Chinese</a>, Korean is not tonal, so you don't need to worry about changing your pitch to get the meaning right.  The bad news is that Korean has a few too many vowels for comfort and small distinctions between many consonants, so pronouncing things exactly right is still a bit of a challenge.</p>

<p id=""mwRQ"">This phrasebook uses the <b id=""mwRg"">Revised Romanization of Korean</b>, which is overwhelmingly the most popular system in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/South_Korea"" title=""South Korea"" id=""mwRw"">South Korea</a>. The <b id=""mwSA"">McCune-Reischauer romanization</b>, used in North Korea and older South Korean texts, is noted in parentheses when different.</p>

<section data-mw-section-id=""3"" id=""mwSQ"" aria-labelledby=""Vowels""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Vowels"">Vowels</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Korean_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3"" title=""Edit section: Vowels""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwSg"">Korean vowels can be short or long, but this is not indicated in writing and the distinction rarely if ever affects meaning. (example: 밤 bam, pronounced short means ""night"", pronounced long means ""chestnut"")</p>

<dl id=""mwSw""><dt id=""mwTA"">a ㅏ</dt><dd id=""mwTQ"">like 'a' in ""f<b id=""mwTg"">a</b>ther""</dd>
<dt id=""mwTw"">o ㅗ</dt><dd id=""mwUA"">like 'o' in ""t<b id=""mwUQ"">o</b>ne""</dd>
<dt id=""mwUg"">eo (ŏ) ㅓ</dt><dd id=""mwUw"">like the ""uh"" in ""lust""</dd>
<dt id=""mwVA"">u ㅜ</dt><dd id=""mwVQ"">A low sound of ""oo"" as in ""hoop"". ""woo"" (Korean does not distinguish between ""oo"" and ""woo"").</dd>
<dt id=""mwVg"">eu (ŭ) ㅡ</dt><dd id=""mwVw"">like 'i' in ""cous<b id=""mwWA"">i</b>n"", ""dozen"". Like the Turkish ""ı"", Polish ""y"" or Russian ""ы"". Kind of similar to the French ""eu"", but as a clearer, purer vowel sound.</dd>
<dt id=""mwWQ"">i ㅣ</dt><dd id=""mwWg"">like the 'i' in ""sh<b id=""mwWw"">i</b>p"" (short) OR the 'ee' in ""sh<b id=""mwXA"">ee</b>p"" (long)</dd>
<dt id=""mwXQ"">e ㅔ</dt><dd id=""mwXg"">like the 'e' in ""b<b id=""mwXw"">e</b>d""</dd>
<dt id=""mwYA"">ae ㅐ</dt><dd id=""mwYQ"">similar to the ""a"" in ""hand"", ""valve"", ""gas"", and ""can""</dd></dl>

<ul id=""mwYg""><li id=""mwYw"">note: ㅐ ae is now virtually identically pronounced as ㅔ e. Only rare words are unconsciously pronounced differently like they were half a century ago (""애"", or ""child"" is one such remnant).</li></ul>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""4"" id=""mwZA"" aria-labelledby=""Common_diphthongs""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Common_diphthongs"">Common diphthongs</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Korean_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4"" title=""Edit section: Common diphthongs""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwZQ"">Korean has two standalone diphthongs:</p>

<dl id=""mwZg""><dt id=""mwZw"">oe ㅚ</dt><dd id=""mwaA"">like 'we' in 'west' (it used to be a different sound, now pronounced the same as ㅞ shown below)</dd>
<dt id=""mwaQ"">ui ㅢ</dt><dd id=""mwag"">like 'ŭ' + 'i'</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwaw"">In addition, most vowels can be modified by prefixing them with 'y' or 'w':</p>

<dl id=""mwbA""><dt id=""mwbQ"">wa ㅘ</dt><dd id=""mwbg"">like 'wa' sound in ""s<b id=""mwbw"">ua</b>ve""</dd>
<dt id=""mwcA"">wae ㅙ</dt><dd id=""mwcQ"">like 'wa' in ""<b id=""mwcg"">wa</b>gon"". Some would argue there is virtually no difference anymore from ㅞ.</dd>
<dt id=""mwcw"">wo ㅝ</dt><dd id=""mwdA"">like 'wuh' sound in ""<b id=""mwdQ"">wo</b>nder""</dd>
<dt id=""mwdg"">wi ㅟ</dt><dd id=""mwdw"">like ""<b id=""mweA"">we</b>"" or 'e' in ""sh<b id=""mweQ"">e</b>"" with rounded lips</dd>
<dt id=""mweg"">we ㅞ</dt><dd id=""mwew"">like 'we' in ""<b id=""mwfA"">we</b>st""</dd>
<dt id=""mwfQ"">ya ㅑ</dt><dd id=""mwfg"">like 'ya' in ""<b id=""mwfw"">ya</b>rd""</dd>
<dt id=""mwgA"">yo ㅛ</dt><dd id=""mwgQ"">like 'yo' in ""<b id=""mwgg"">yo</b>semite"" or ""New <b id=""mwgw"">Yo</b>rk"". <b id=""mwhA"">Not</b> like ""<b id=""mwhQ"">yaw</b>"" or ""<b id=""mwhg"">yo</b>ke"".</dd>
<dt id=""mwhw"">yeo (yŏ) ㅕ</dt><dd id=""mwiA"">like 'you' in ""<b id=""mwiQ"">you</b>ng""</dd>
<dt id=""mwig"">yu ㅠ</dt><dd id=""mwiw"">like ""<b id=""mwjA"">you</b>""</dd>
<dt id=""mwjQ"">ye ㅖ</dt><dd id=""mwjg"">like 'ye' in ""<b id=""mwjw"">ye</b>s""</dd>
<dt id=""mwkA"">yae ㅒ</dt><dd id=""mwkQ"">like 'ye' in ""<b id=""mwkg"">ye</b>s""; it has virtually assimiliated to be the same as 'ㅖ'</dd></dl>

<ul id=""mwkw""><li id=""mwlA"">to summarize the assimilated vowel diphthongs mentioned above,</li></ul>
<p id=""mwlQ"">ㅙ = ㅚ = ㅞ = 'we' in ""<b id=""mwlg"">we</b>st""
ㅖ = ㅒ = 'ye' in ""<b id=""mwlw"">ye</b>s""</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""5"" id=""mwmA"" aria-labelledby=""Consonants""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Consonants"">Consonants</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Korean_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5"" title=""Edit section: Consonants""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwmQ"">Most Korean consonants come in three versions, namely <b id=""mwmg"">unaspirated</b> (without a puff of air), <b id=""mwmw"">aspirated</b> (with a puff of air) and <b id=""mwnA"">tensed</b> (stressed).  Unaspirated consonants exist in English too, but never alone: compare the sound of 'p' in ""pot"" (aspirated) and ""spot"" (unaspirated). Many English speakers find it helpful to pronounce an imperceptible little ""m"" in front to 'stop' the puff.  Tensing isn't really found in English, but pronouncing the consonant quick and hard is a reasonable substitute.</p>

<dl id=""mwnQ""><dt id=""mwng"">b (p) ㅂ</dt><dd id=""mwnw"">like 'p' in ""spit"" (unaspirated)</dd>
<dt id=""mwoA"">p (p', ph) ㅍ</dt><dd id=""mwoQ"">like 'p' in ""pig"" (aspirated)</dd>
<dt id=""mwog"">pp ㅃ</dt><dd id=""mwow"">tensed 'p', like 'p' in ""petit"" in French</dd></dl>

<dl id=""mwpA""><dt id=""mwpQ"">d (t) ㄷ</dt><dd id=""mwpg"">like 't' in ""stab"" (unaspirated)</dd>
<dt id=""mwpw"">t (t', th) ㅌ</dt><dd id=""mwqA"">like 't' in ""top"" (aspirated)</dd>
<dt id=""mwqQ"">tt ㄸ</dt><dd id=""mwqg"">tensed 't'</dd></dl>

<dl id=""mwqw""><dt id=""mwrA"">g (k) ㄱ</dt><dd id=""mwrQ"">like 'k' in ""skate"" (unaspirated)</dd>
<dt id=""mwrg"">k (k', k) ㅋ</dt><dd id=""mwrw"">like 'c' in ""cat"" (aspirated)</dd>
<dt id=""mwsA"">kk ㄲ</dt><dd id=""mwsQ"">tensed 'k'</dd></dl>

<dl id=""mwsg""><dt id=""mwsw"">j (ch) ㅈ</dt><dd id=""mwtA"">like 'g' in ""gin"" (unaspirated)</dd>
<dt id=""mwtQ"">ch (ch') ㅊ</dt><dd id=""mwtg"">like 'ch' in ""chin"" (aspirated). Usually pronounced as a light aspirated 't' as a final consonant</dd>
<dt id=""mwtw"">jj ㅉ</dt><dd id=""mwuA"">tensed 'j'</dd></dl>

<dl id=""mwuQ""><dt id=""mwug"">s ㅅ</dt><dd id=""mwuw"">like 's' in ""soon"", 'sh' before <i id=""mwvA"">i</i> or any ""y"" diphthong. Usually pronounced as a very light 't' as a final consonant</dd>
<dt id=""mwvQ"">ss ㅆ</dt><dd id=""mwvg"">tensed 's', 's' in 'sea', never 'sh'</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwvw"">Standalone consonants:</p>
<dl id=""mwwA""><dt id=""mwwQ"">n ㄴ</dt><dd id=""mwwg"">like 'n' in ""nice""</dd>
<dt id=""mwww"">m ㅁ</dt><dd id=""mwxA"">like 'm' in ""mother""</dd>
<dt id=""mwxQ"">l ㄹ</dt><dd id=""mwxg"">somewhere between 'l', 'r' and 'n', original sound is 'r' or 'l'. and 'n' sound occurs through initial consonant mutation.</dd>
<dt id=""mwxw"">h ㅎ</dt><dd id=""mwyA"">like 'h' in ""help""</dd></dl>

<dl id=""mwyQ""><dt id=""mwyg"">ng ㅇ</dt><dd id=""mwyw"">like 'ng' in ""sing"". Unpronounced (placeholder) when at the start of a syllable.</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwzA"">While the rules above are usually correct for the first consonant, those in the middle of a word are usually (but not always) <b id=""mwzQ"">voiced</b>, which means that ㅂㄷㅈㄱ turn into English ""b"", ""d"", ""j"" and ""k"".  The best rule of thumb is to concentrate on remembering that the first consonant is ""special"" and the rest are more or less as in English: <i id=""mwzg"">bibimbap</i> (비빔밥) is pronounced ""<i id=""mwzw"">pee</i>-bim-bap"", not ""<i id=""mw0A"">bee</i>-bim-bap"" or ""<i id=""mw0Q"">p'ee</i>-bim-bap"". Also, if one syllable ends with a consonant other than ""ng"" and is immediately followed by one that starts with a vowel, similar to <i id=""mw0g"">liaison</i> in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/French_phrasebook"" title=""French phrasebook"" id=""mw0w"">French</a>, the final consonant of the first syllable is instead pronounced as if it were the initial consonant of the second syllable. If a syllable ends with ""h"" and is preceded by a syllable that ends in a consonant, the ""h"" is dropped, and the ending consonant of the previous syllable is pronounced as if it were the initial consonant of the second syllable.</p>

<p id=""mw1A"">The aspirated spellings with ""h"" are used only in the official North Korean orthography.</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""6"" id=""mw1Q"" aria-labelledby=""Loanwords""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Loanwords"">Loanwords</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Korean_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6"" title=""Edit section: Loanwords""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mw1g"">Native Korean words can end only in vowels or the consonants <i id=""mw1w"">k</i>, <i id=""mw2A"">l</i>, <i id=""mw2Q"">m</i>, <i id=""mw2g"">n</i>, <i id=""mw2w"">ng</i>, <i id=""mw3A"">p</i> or <i id=""mw3Q"">t</i>, and any words imported into Korean are shoehorned to fit this pattern, usually by padding any errant consonants with the vowel <i id=""mw3g"">eu</i> (ㅡ).  For example, any English word ending in ""t"" will be pronounced as <i id=""mw3w"">teu</i> (트) in Korean, eg. <i id=""mw4A"">Baeteumaen</i> (배트맨) for ""Batman"".  In addition, the English sound ""f"" is turned into <i id=""mw4Q"">p</i> and has that vowel tacked on, so ""golf"" becomes <i id=""mw4g"">golpeu</i> (골프).</p>

<p id=""mw5g"">Korean is generally written using a native alphabet known as <b id=""mw5w"">hangul</b> (<b id=""mw6A"">chosongul</b> in North Korea and China). Designed by King Sejong the Great of the Joseon Dynasty in an attempt to increase literacy rates and rather scary-looking at first, it's in fact a very logical alphabetic writing system <i id=""mw6Q"">far</i> easier than Chinese characters or even the Japanese <i id=""mw6g"">kana</i> syllabary, and it's well worth putting in the time to learn them if staying in Korea for more than a day or two.</p>

<p id=""mw6w"">The basic idea is simple: hangul consists of letters called <i id=""mw7A"">jamo</i> combined into square blocks, where each block represents a syllable.  The block is always in the order (consonant)-vowel-(consonant), stacked from top to bottom, where ㅇ is used as the first jamo if the first consonant is missing, and the space for the last consonant can be left empty is missing.  For example, the word <i id=""mw7Q"">Seoul</i> (서울) consists of the syllables <i id=""mw7g"">seo</i> (ㅅ <i id=""mw7w"">s</i> plus ㅓ <i id=""mw8A"">eo</i>, no final consonant) and <i id=""mw8Q"">ul</i> (ㅇ plus ㅜ <i id=""mw8g"">u</i> plus ㄹ <i id=""mw8w"">l</i>).  Tensed consonants are created by doubling the jamo
(ㅅ <i id=""mw9A"">s</i> → ㅆ <i id=""mw9Q"">ss</i>) and <i id=""mw9g"">y</i>-vowel diphthongs have an extra dash tacked on (ㅏ <i id=""mw9w"">a</i> → ㅑ <i id=""mw-A"">ya</i>).  And that's pretty much it!</p>

<p id=""mw-Q"">Classical Chinese was the literary language in Korea until the fall of the Joseon Dynasty in 1910, and many Korean words can also be written using Chinese characters, known as <b id=""mw-g"">hanja</b> in Korean. These were abolished in North Korea in 1949 and in South Korea in 1968, though they may still show up in restaurant menus and signs as an aesthetic choice. Otherwise, the few times you might encounter them are in brackets next to the hangul to describe an unfamiliar term or distinguish a term from a homophone, in some newspaper headlines, and in some legal documents.</p>

<p id=""mw_A"">While Chinese characters are seldom written, many words themselves are simply Chinese loan words written as they are pronounced — not according to the Mandarin pronunciation, but according to the standardized Korean pronunciation of those same characters used in Chinese. Like Greek and Latin words in English, Chinese words are often found in the more formal and less vernacular sciences, and even more so with 19th-century new Chinese words coined by the Japanese, and used in both Korea and China. Japanese, Vietnamese, and Chinese speakers may find some familiarity with some of these overlapping Chinese terms, although pronunciations are different and Koreans only write out sounds and not the original Chinese characters. Although not nearly as much as Cantonese, Korean pronunciation of Chinese words retains more medieval Chinese pronunciations of the Tang dynasty some 1300 years ago, than the Northeast dialect-influenced modern Mandarin.</p>

<p id=""mw_Q"">Due to American influences since the end of World War II, by far the largest number of non-Chinese loan words in modern Korean as spoken in South Korea come from American English. The Korean words for many modern concepts are essentially the same as the English ones (eg. hotel (호텔), taxi (택시), computer (컴퓨터)), but will be written in hangul. If you can learn how to read hangul, as an English speaker, you will find it surprisingly easy to read many signs.</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""8"" id=""mw_g"" aria-labelledby=""Regional_differences""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading2""><h2 id=""Regional_differences"">Regional differences</h2><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Korean_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8"" title=""Edit section: Regional differences""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mw_w"">The official pronunciation of words differs somewhat between North Korea and South Korea. One noticeable difference is that for Sino-Korean words, the initial ""r"" in North Korea typically gets dropped in South Korea when before an ""i"" or ""y"", and becomes an initial ""n"" otherwise, so Korean cold noodles is known as 랭면 <i id=""mwAQA"">raengmyeon</i> in North Korea, but 냉면 <i id=""mwAQE"">naengmyeon</i> in South Korea, while the common Korean surname typically transliterated as ""Lee"" in English is 리 <i id=""mwAQI"">Ri</i> in North Korea, but 이 <i id=""mwAQM"">Yi</i> in South Korea. The initial ""n"" in North Korea gets dropped when before and ""i"" or ""y"" in South Korea, so the word for ""woman"" is 녀자 <i id=""mwAQQ"">nyeoja</i> in North Korea, but 여자 <i id=""mwAQU"">yeoja</i> in South Korea.</p>

<p id=""mwAQY"">In addition to differences in accent, there are some vocabulary differences in the standard form of Korean language between South Korea and North Korea. One of the most noticeable differences is that South Koreans use many loan words from American English due to heavy American influences, while North Koreans will usually coin their own terms, or use loan words from other languages (especially Russian) instead. Some loanwords from a common origin are transliterated differently in the two standard forms. For example ""computer"" is ""컴퓨터"" (<i id=""mwAQc"">keompyuteo</i>) in South Korea and ""콤퓨터"" (<i id=""mwAQg"">kompyuteo</i>) in North Korea.</p>

<p id=""mwAQk"">Ethnic Koreans from China generally follow the North Korean standard, though they are more likely than North Koreans to understand South Korean terms due to the popularity of South Korean dramas and pop culture in China. In addition, many loan words from Mandarin have been incorporated into the Korean language in China.</p>

<p id=""mwATo"">Another noticeable difference between the Korean language in South Korea, and that among the ethnic Korean minority in China is the way in which Chinese place names and personal names are translated. While South Koreans phonetically transliterate the Standard Mandarin names into hangul, ethnic Koreans from China instead use the conventional Sino-Korean readings of the characters that make up their Chinese names. For example, <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Beijing"" title=""Beijing"" id=""mwATs"">Beijing</a> is known to South Koreans as 베이징 (<i id=""mwATw"">Beijing</i>) but to ethnic Koreans from China as 북경 (<i id=""mwAT0"">Bukgyeong</i>), while <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Shanghai"" title=""Shanghai"" id=""mwAT4"">Shanghai</a> is known to South Koreans as 상하이 (<i id=""mwAT8"">Sanghai</i>) but to ethnic Koreans from China as 상해 (<i id=""mwAUA"">Sanghae</i>). Therefore, Chinese cities with large ethnic Korean communities usually have a different local Korean name from the South Korean name; for instance, <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Yanji"" title=""Yanji"" id=""mwAUE"">Yanji</a> is known as 연길 (<i id=""mwAUI"">Yeon-gil</i>) to the local ethnic Koreans, but 옌지 (<i id=""mwAUM"">Yenji</i>) to South Koreans. As an example for personal names, Chinese president Xi Jinping is 시진핑 (<i id=""mwAUQ"">Si Jin-ping</i>) to South Koreans but 습근평 (<i id=""mwAUU"">Seup Geun-pyeong</i>) to ethnic Koreans from China.</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""12"" id=""mwAhI"" aria-labelledby=""Numbers""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Numbers"">Numbers</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Korean_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12"" title=""Edit section: Numbers""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwAhM"">Korean has two sets of numbers, namely <b id=""mwAhQ"">native</b> Korean numbers and <b id=""mwAhU"">Sino-Korean</b> numbers (which are borrowed from <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Chinese_phrasebook"" title=""Chinese phrasebook"" id=""mwAhY"">Chinese</a>). <span class=""mw-empty-elt"" about=""#mwt12"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwApE"" data-mw=""{&quot;parts&quot;:[{&quot;template&quot;:{&quot;target&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;infobox&quot;,&quot;href&quot;:&quot;./Template:Infobox&quot;},&quot;params&quot;:{&quot;1&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;Counting words&quot;},&quot;2&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;Similar to Chinese and Japanese, Korean uses '''counter''' words when counting objects. <div style=""font-weight:bold;"">Counting words</div>

<p>Similar to Chinese and Japanese, Korean uses <b>counter</b> words when counting objects. For example, ""two beers"" is <i>maekju dubyeong</i> (맥주 2병), where <i>du</i> is ""two"" and <i>-byeong</i> means ""bottles"". There are many counters, but the most useful ones are <i>myeong</i> (명) for people, <i>jang</i> (장) for papers including tickets, and <i>gae</i> (개) for pretty much anything else (which is not always strictly correct, but will usually be understood and is growing in colloquial usage).</p>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id=""36"" id=""mwBqo"" aria-labelledby=""Learning_more""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading2""><h2 id=""Learning_more"">Learning more</h2><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Korean_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=36"" title=""Edit section: Learning more""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwBqs"">For those wishing to assess their Korean language proficiency, the main proficiency test is the Test of Proficiency in Korean (TOPIK). It assesses the language skills of non-native speakers in reading, writing, and listening. TOPIK is administered by the National Institute of International Education (NIIED). The test is divided into two main levels (TOPIK I and TOPIK II), with subdivisions into six levels.</p>
