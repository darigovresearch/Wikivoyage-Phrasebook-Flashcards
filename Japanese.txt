<p id=""mwDA""><b id=""mwDQ"">Japanese</b> (<span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt5"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwDg"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""日本語""}},""i"":0}}]}'>日本語</span> <i id=""mwDw"">nihongo</i>) is spoken in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Japan"" title=""Japan"" id=""mwEA"">Japan</a>, and essentially nowhere else other than areas of Micronesia, <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Brazil"" title=""Brazil"" id=""mwEQ"">Brazil</a>, <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Peru"" title=""Peru"" id=""mwEg"">Peru</a>, the <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/United_States_of_America"" title=""United States of America"" class=""mw-redirect"" id=""mwEw"">United States</a> and <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Taiwan"" title=""Taiwan"" id=""mwFA"">Taiwan</a> where there is a long-established Japanese diaspora, with sizeable Japanese-speaking expatriate communities in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/South_Korea"" title=""South Korea"" id=""mwFQ"">South Korea</a>, <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/China"" title=""China"" id=""mwFg"">China</a> and <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Singapore"" title=""Singapore"" id=""mwFw"">Singapore</a>. Japanese may be distantly related to <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Korean_phrasebook"" title=""Korean phrasebook"" id=""mwGA"">Korean</a>, but is entirely unrelated to <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Chinese_phrasebook"" title=""Chinese phrasebook"" id=""mwGQ"">Chinese</a>, though it uses a large amount of imported Chinese vocabulary. The written form uses a combination of Katakana, Hiragana and Kanji characters which were all derived from Chinese characters.</p>

<p id=""mwGg"">The <b id=""mwGw""><a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Japanese_phrasebook"" class=""mw-selflink selflink"" id=""mwHA"">Japanese</a></b> language has several distinct dialects. Standard Japanese (<i id=""mwHQ"">hyōjungo</i> 標準語), which is based on the Tokyo dialect, is taught in schools and understood by all people throughout the country. The slang-heavy dialect of the <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Kansai"" title=""Kansai"" id=""mwHg"">Kansai</a> region is particularly famous in Japanese pop culture, while the northern <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Tohoku"" title=""Tohoku"" id=""mwHw"">Tohoku</a> region and southern <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Kyushu"" title=""Kyushu"" id=""mwIA"">Kyushu</a> are famous for their impenetrable dialects. On the southern islands of <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Okinawa"" title=""Okinawa"" id=""mwIQ"">Okinawa</a>, many of the closely related <b id=""mwIg"">Ryukyuan</b> languages are spoken, mostly by the elderly, while many locals speak Okinawan Japanese, a dialect that borrows much vocabulary from Ryukyuan languages. In northern <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Hokkaido"" title=""Hokkaido"" id=""mwIw"">Hokkaido</a>, a rare few still speak <b id=""mwJA"">Ainu</b>.</p>

<meta property=""mw:PageProp/toc"" id=""mwJQ"" data-mw='{""autoGenerated"":true}'/></section><section data-mw-section-id=""1"" id=""mwJg"" aria-labelledby=""Pronunciation_guide""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading2""><h2 id=""Pronunciation_guide"">Pronunciation guide</h2><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1"" title=""Edit section: Pronunciation guide""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwJw"">Japanese is not a tonal language like Chinese or Thai. The vowels are pronounced virtually identical to the ""Italian way"" and there are very few consonants that do not exist in English. All syllables are to be pronounced equal in length. Long vowels take the length of two syllables. Combinations like <i id=""mwKA"">kya</i> are treated like one syllable and are the only occurrence of glides (semivowels), all other syllables are to be pronounced rather separately.</p>

<p id=""mwKQ"">Also avoid placing too much emphasis on particular words or syllables. Although Japanese does have a form of stress and intonation, it is significantly flatter than English. Word stress is much more subtle and neglecting it at this point should not interfere with meaning. Trying to keep your intonation flat will make your attempts to speak Japanese more comprehensible to local listeners. When asking questions, you can raise the tone at the end, as in English.</p>

<section data-mw-section-id=""2"" id=""mwKg"" aria-labelledby=""Vowels""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Vowels"">Vowels</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2"" title=""Edit section: Vowels""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwKw"">Japanese has only five basic vowels, but the distinction between short and long vowels is important. For example, 少女 (<i id=""mwLA"">shōjo</i>, with a long first vowel) means ""teenage girl"", while 処女 (<i id=""mwLQ"">shojo</i>, with a short first vowel) means ""virgin woman"". Even when it comes to proper nouns, you will not be understood if you do not distinguish the long and short vowels correctly. The sounds below are first given in romanized Japanese, then hiragana and finally katakana.</p>

<p id=""mwLg"">The <b id=""mwLw"">short vowels</b> are:</p>
<dl id=""mwMA""><dt id=""mwMQ"">a, あ, ア</dt><dd id=""mwMg"">like 'a' in ""p<b id=""mwMw"">a</b>lm""</dd>
<dt id=""mwNA"">i, い, イ</dt><dd id=""mwNQ"">like 'i' in ""mar<b id=""mwNg"">i</b>ne""</dd>
<dt id=""mwNw"">u, う, ウ</dt><dd id=""mwOA"">like 'oo' in ""h<b id=""mwOQ"">oo</b>p"", but short (said without rounded lips)</dd>
<dt id=""mwOg"">e, え, エ</dt><dd id=""mwOw"">like 'e' in ""s<b id=""mwPA"">e</b>t""</dd>
<dt id=""mwPQ"">o, お, オ</dt><dd id=""mwPg"">like 'o' in ""r<b id=""mwPw"">o</b>pe"", but less round</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwQA"">Note that ""u"" is often weak at the end of syllables.  In particular, the common endings <i id=""mwQQ"">desu</i> and <i id=""mwQg"">masu</i> are usually pronounced as <i id=""mwQw"">des</i> and <i id=""mwRA"">mas</i> respectively. Also, the kana ""do"" and ""to"" are sometimes pronounced with a weak ""o"".</p>

<p id=""mwRQ"">The <b id=""mwRg"">long vowels</b> are generally the same sound as the short vowels, only held approximately 60% longer. The long vowels, marked with a macron (¯) or by two adjacent vowels, are:</p>
<dl id=""mwRw""><dt id=""mwSA"">ā, ああ, アー</dt><dd id=""mwSQ"">like 'a' in ""f<b id=""mwSg"">a</b>ther""</dd>
<dt id=""mwSw"">ii or ī, いい, イー</dt><dd id=""mwTA"">like 'ee' in ""ch<b id=""mwTQ"">ee</b>se""</dd>
<dt id=""mwTg"">ū, うう, ウー</dt><dd id=""mwTw"">like 'oo' in ""h<b id=""mwUA"">oo</b>p""</dd>
<dt id=""mwUQ"">ei or ē, えい, ええ, エー</dt><dd id=""mwUg"">like the 'ay' in ""p<b id=""mwUw"">ay</b>""</dd>
<dt id=""mwVA"">ō, おお, おう, オー</dt><dd id=""mwVQ"">stretch out the 'o' in ""s<b id=""mwVg"">o</b>ap""</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwVw"">All descriptions above are approximations, it's best to practice with a native speaker.</p>

<p id=""mwWg"">With the solitary exception of ""n"" (ん・ン), consonants in Japanese are always followed by a vowel to form a syllable. Consonants and vowels are not freely combinable as in English, see table on the right for all possible syllables and note irregularities like し <i id=""mwWw"">shi</i> or ふ <i id=""mwXA"">fu</i>. Certain syllables can be marked with diacritics, which alters the pronunciation of the consonant part. The list below first gives the consonant part of the syllable in romanized Japanese, then the Japanese syllables that the sound occurs in first in Hiragana, then Katakana.</p>

<dl id=""mwXQ""><dt id=""mwXg"">k in かきくけこ・カキクケコ (ka ki ku ke ko)</dt><dd id=""mwXw"">like 'k' in ""king""</dd>
<dt id=""mwYA"">g in がぎぐげご・ガギグゲゴ (ga gi gu ge go)</dt><dd id=""mwYQ"">like 'g' in ""go""</dd>
<dt id=""mwYg"">s in さすせそ・サスセソ (sa su se so)</dt><dd id=""mwYw"">like 's' in ""sit""</dd>

<dt id=""mwZg"">t in たてと・タテト (ta te to)</dt><dd id=""mwZw"">like 't' in ""top""</dd>
<dt id=""mwaA"">d in だでど・ダデド (da de do)</dt><dd id=""mwaQ"">like 'd' in ""dog""</dd>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""4"" id=""mwiA"" aria-labelledby=""Small_symbols""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Small_symbols"">Small symbols</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4"" title=""Edit section: Small symbols""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwiQ"">The symbols つ・ツ and やゆよ・ヤユヨ, as well as あいうえお・アイウエオ can be written in a smaller size. In the latter group, this is almost always seen only in katakana, as it is only used for loan words. The small つ・ツ indicates a glottal stop, as listed below, while for the rest of the symbols, indicates that they are to be pronounced as part of the same syllable of the preceding symbol. For instance, in the loan word フォーク (<i id=""mwig"">fōku</i>), from English ""fork"", note the smaller size of the オ, which is why it is <i id=""mwiw"">not</i> pronounced <i id=""mwjA"">fuōku</i>. To highlight the importance of this distinction, びょういん (<i id=""mwjQ"">byōin</i>, with the small よ) means hospital, and びよういん (<i id=""mwjg"">biyōin</i>, with the big よ pronounced as a separate syllable from the び) means beauty salon.</p>

<dl id=""mwjw""><dt id=""mwkA"">っ・ッ (small <i id=""mwkQ"">tsu</i>)</dt><dd id=""mwkg"">glottal stop; the following consonant is prepared, held and stopped for the duration of one syllable. For example, にっぽん <i id=""mwkw"">nippon</i> is pronounced ""nip-(pause)-pon"".  (Note that the double consonants <i id=""mwlA"">nn, mm</i>, which are not written with っ, do not have this pause.)</dd></dl>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id=""6"" id=""mwpg"" aria-labelledby=""Grammar""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading2""><h2 id=""Grammar"">Grammar</h2><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6"" title=""Edit section: Grammar""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>

<p id=""mwpw"">Japanese sentence structure is very similar to that of <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Korean_phrasebook"" title=""Korean phrasebook"" id=""mwqA"">Korean</a>, so speakers of Korean will find many aspects of Japanese grammar familiar, and vice versa.</p>

<p id=""mwqQ"">Unlike English, which uses the <i id=""mwqg"">subject-verb-object</i> syntax, Japanese syntax is <i id=""mwqw"">subject-object-verb</i>. Japanese uses postpositions instead of prepositions (<i id=""mwrA"">Japan in</i> and not <i id=""mwrQ"">in Japan</i>). However, unlike many European languages, it has no gender, declensions or plurals. Nouns never decline while adjectives follow a generally standard conjugation pattern. However, verbs have extensive conjugation patterns and many Japanese lessons for foreign language learners are about getting these conjugations right. Verbs and adjectives also conjugate by politeness level, though, and in a rather peculiar way.</p>

<p id=""mwrg"">Japanese is an agglutinative language, meaning several morphemes which have purely grammatical functions are glued to the end of a word stem to express the grammatical function. The more the intended meaning differs from the basic form of the word, the more morphemes are glued together.</p>

<section data-mw-section-id=""7"" id=""mw-w"" aria-labelledby=""Forming_sentences""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Forming_sentences"">Forming sentences</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7"" title=""Edit section: Forming sentences""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>

<span class=""mw-empty-elt"" about=""#mwt8"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mw_A"" data-mw=""{&quot;parts&quot;:[{&quot;template&quot;:{&quot;target&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;infobox&quot;,&quot;href&quot;:&quot;./Template:Infobox&quot;},&quot;params&quot;:{&quot;1&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;Particle pronunciation&quot;},&quot;2&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;The hiragana syllables '''は ''ha''''', '''へ ''he''''' and '''を ''wo''''' are pronounced as '''''wa''''', '''''e''''' and '''''o''''' respectively when used as a particle.&quot;}},&quot;i&quot;:0}}]}""><link rel=""mw-deduplicated-inline-style"" href=""mw-data:TemplateStyles:r5162413"" about=""#mwt9"" typeof=""mw:Extension/templatestyles"" data-mw='{""name"":""templatestyles"",""attrs"":{""src"":""Template:Infobox/styles.css""}}'/></span><table class=""pp_infobox infobox"" style=""float:right; clear:right; margin-left: 1em; width:40%;"" cellpadding=""8"" cellspacing=""1"" about=""#mwt8"">
<tbody><tr><td>
<div style=""font-weight:bold;"">Particle pronunciation</div>

<p>The hiragana syllables <b>は <i>ha</i></b>, <b>へ <i>he</i></b> and <b>を <i>wo</i></b> are pronounced as <i><b>wa</b></i>, <i><b>e</b></i> and <i><b>o</b></i> respectively when used as a particle.</p></td></tr>
</tbody></table>

<p id=""mw_Q"">Japanese grammar generally employs a subject-object-verb order, but is very modular and flexible since the grammatical meaning of a word is expressed by the morphemes glued to its end and special marker particles. The two most important particles are the topic marker は <i id=""mw_g"">wa</i> and the object marker を <i id=""mw_w"">o</i>.</p>

<dl id=""mwAQA""><dd id=""mwAQE"">I saw the movie.</dd>
<dd id=""mwAQI""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt10"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAQM"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""私&apos;&apos;&apos;は&apos;&apos;&apos;映画&apos;&apos;&apos;を&apos;&apos;&apos;見ました。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>私<b>は</b>映画<b>を</b>見ました。</span></dd>
<dd id=""mwAQQ""><i id=""mwAQU"">Watashi-<b id=""mwAQY"">wa</b> eiga-<b id=""mwAQc"">o</b> mimashita.</i></dd>
<dd id=""mwAQg""><i id=""mwAQk"">I-[<b id=""mwAQo"">topic</b>] movie-[<b id=""mwAQs"">object</b>] seen.</i></dd></dl>

<p id=""mwAQw"">It becomes a little more complicated if both objects and subjects are mixed within a sentence and the subject marker が <i id=""mwAQ0"">ga</i> is thrown in.</p>

<dl id=""mwAQ4""><dd id=""mwAQ8"">I discovered that she likes tea.</dd>
<dd id=""mwARA""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt11"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwARE"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""私&apos;&apos;&apos;は&apos;&apos;&apos;彼女&apos;&apos;&apos;が&apos;&apos;&apos;お茶&apos;&apos;&apos;を&apos;&apos;&apos;好きな事&apos;&apos;&apos;が&apos;&apos;&apos;分かった。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>私<b>は</b>彼女<b>が</b>お茶<b>を</b>好きな事<b>が</b>分かった。</span><br id=""mwARI""/><i id=""mwARM"">Watashi-<b id=""mwARQ"">wa</b> kanojo-<b id=""mwARU"">ga</b> ocha-<b id=""mwARY"">o</b> sukinakoto-<b id=""mwARc"">ga</b> wakatta.</i></dd>
<dd id=""mwARg""><i id=""mwARk"">I-[<b id=""mwARo"">topic</b>] she-[<b id=""mwARs"">subj.</b>] tea-[<b id=""mwARw"">obj.</b>] like-[<b id=""mwAR0"">subj.</b>] understood.</i></dd></dl>

<p id=""mwAR4"">Students of the language can spend years wrapping their heads around the difference between the <i id=""mwAR8"">topic</i> of a sentence (marked by は <i id=""mwASA"">wa</i>) and the <i id=""mwASE"">subject</i> of a sentence (marked by が <i id=""mwASI"">ga</i>).  However, as a beginner, you can fairly safely always use は <i id=""mwASM"">wa</i> to mark the person doing the action and get your message across.</p>

<p id=""mwASQ"">Some other useful particles are:</p>

<dl id=""mwASU""><dt id=""mwASY"">の no</dt><dd id=""mwASc"">possessive marker</dd>
<dd id=""mwASg"">The mother's child</dd>
<dd id=""mwASk""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt12"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwASo"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""母&apos;&apos;&apos;の&apos;&apos;&apos;子""}},""i"":0}}]}'>母<b>の</b>子</span></dd>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""8"" id=""mwAWo"" aria-labelledby='The_verb_""to_be""'><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id='The_verb_""to_be""'><span id=""The_verb_.22to_be.22"" typeof=""mw:FallbackId""></span>The verb ""to be""</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8"" title='Edit section: The verb ""to be""'><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwAWs"">Japanese does not have an exact equivalent to the English verb ""to be"".  Instead, the easiest way to form <b id=""mwAWw"">""A equals B""</b> type expressions like ""I am ..."" or ""This is ..."" is the pattern <b id=""mwAW0"">A <i id=""mwAW4"">wa</i>, B <i id=""mwAW8"">desu</i></b>.</p>

<dl id=""mwAXA""><dd id=""mwAXE""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt21"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAXI"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""私は、山田です。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>私は、山田です。</span> <i id=""mwAXM"">Watashi wa, Yamada desu</i> (""I [am] Yamada."")</dd>
<dd id=""mwAXQ""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt22"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAXU"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""これは、りんごです。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>これは、りんごです。</span> <i id=""mwAXY"">Kore wa, ringo desu</i> (""This [is] apple."")</dd>
<dd id=""mwAXc""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt23"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAXg"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""それは、赤いです。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>それは、赤いです。</span> <i id=""mwAXk"">Sore wa, akai desu</i> (""That [is] red."").</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwAXo"">The word です　<i id=""mwAXs"">desu</i> here is <i id=""mwAXw"">not</i> a verb, it's a polite copula (linking word), which can be omitted in colloquial speech or replaced with other copulas including でした <i id=""mwAX0"">deshita</i> (polite past), でしょう <i id=""mwAX4"">deshō</i> (polite suggestion) or だ <i id=""mwAX8"">da</i> (plain).  The topic indicated by は <i id=""mwAYA"">wa</i> is also optional and is often implied by context:</p>

<dl id=""mwAYE""><dd id=""mwAYI""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt24"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAYM"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""あなたはだれですか？""}},""i"":0}}]}'>あなたはだれですか？</span> <i id=""mwAYQ"">Anata wa dare desu ka?</i> (""Who [are] you?"")</dd>
<dd id=""mwAYU""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt25"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAYY"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""山田です。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>山田です。</span> <i id=""mwAYc"">Yamada desu.</i> (""[I am] Yamada."")</dd>
<dd id=""mwAYg""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt26"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAYk"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""これは何ですか？""}},""i"":0}}]}'>これは何ですか？</span> <i id=""mwAYo"">Kore wa nan desu ka?</i> (""What [is] this?"")</dd>
<dd id=""mwAYs""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt27"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAYw"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""りんごです。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>りんごです。</span> <i id=""mwAY0"">Ringo desu</i>. (""[This is] an apple."")</dd>
<dd id=""mwAY4""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt28"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAY8"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""それは何色ですか？""}},""i"":0}}]}'>それは何色ですか？</span> <i id=""mwAZA"">Sore wa nani-iro desu ka?</i> (""What color [is] that?"")<br id=""mwAZE""/></dd>
<dd id=""mwAZI""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt29"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAZM"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""赤いです。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>赤いです。</span> <i id=""mwAZQ"">Akai desu</i>. (""[That is] red."")</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwAZU"">The two verbs いる <i id=""mwAZY"">iru</i> > <i id=""mwAZc"">imasu</i> and ある <i id=""mwAZg"">aru</i> > <i id=""mwAZk"">arimasu</i> express the <b id=""mwAZo"">physical presence</b> of a person or animal in the former case, or an object in the latter case.  To say <b id=""mwAZs"">""A is located in B</b>"", use the pattern <b id=""mwAZw"">A <i id=""mwAZ0"">ga</i> B <i id=""mwAZ4"">ni imasu/arimasu</i> </b>:</p>

<dl id=""mwAZ8""><dd id=""mwAaA""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt30"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAaE"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""山田さんがここにいます。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>山田さんがここにいます。</span> <i id=""mwAaI"">Yamada-san ga koko ni imasu.</i> (""Mr. Yamada is [physically located] here."")</dd>
<dd id=""mwAaM""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt31"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAaQ"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""本が棚にありますか？""}},""i"":0}}]}'>本が棚にありますか？</span>　<i id=""mwAaU"">Hon ga tana ni arimasu ka?</i> (""Is there a book on the shelf?"")</dd>
<dd id=""mwAaY""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt32"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAac"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""はい、あります。""}},""i"":0}}]}'>はい、あります。</span> <i id=""mwAag"">Hai, arimasu.</i> (""Yes, [the book] is [on the shelf]."")</dd></dl>

<span class=""mw-empty-elt"" about=""#mwt33"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAao"" data-mw=""{&quot;parts&quot;:[{&quot;template&quot;:{&quot;target&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;infobox&quot;,&quot;href&quot;:&quot;./Template:Infobox&quot;},&quot;params&quot;:{&quot;1&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;Me, myself and I&quot;},&quot;2&quot;:{&quot;wt&quot;:&quot;As long as you're not 100% sure what you're doing you should always refer to yourself as 私 ''watashi'' and address others by their last name + さん ''san''. <tbody><tr><td>
<div style=""font-weight:bold;"">Me, myself and I</div>

<p>As long as you're not 100% sure what you're doing you should always refer to yourself as 私 <i>watashi</i> and address others by their last name + さん <i>san</i>. If you feel adventurous, here are a number of ways to address people.</p>

<p><b>I</b></p>
<dl><dt><span lang=""ja"">私</span> <i>watashi</i>, <i>watakushi</i></dt><dd>the most common polite form for ""I"", lit. ""private""</dd>
<dt><span lang=""ja"">あたし</span> <i>atashi</i></dt><dd>informal feminine version of <i>watashi</i></dd>
<dt><span lang=""ja"">うち</span> <i>uchi</i></dt><dd>Western dialect form of <i>watashi</i>, lit ""(my) house"", usually used to refer to one's family or home</dd>
<dt><span lang=""ja"">僕</span> <i>boku</i></dt><dd>boyish and more informal, used only by males</dd>
<dt><span lang=""ja"">俺</span> <i>ore</i></dt><dd>male speak (rude)</dd></dl>

<p><b>you</b></p>
<dl><dt><span lang=""ja"">あなた</span> <i>anata</i></dt><dd>most common form for ""you"", not too direct</dd>
<dt><span lang=""ja"">あんた</span> <i>anta</i></dt><dd>more direct, tends to be insulting</dd>
<dt><span lang=""ja"">君</span> <i>kimi</i></dt><dd>more direct, mostly by the older person or from a man to a woman</dd>
<dt><span lang=""ja"">お前</span> <i>omae</i></dt><dd>very direct and informal, used only by males</dd>
<dt><span lang=""ja"">てめえ</span> <i>temē</i></dt><dd><i>very</i> rude, used only by males</dd></dl></td></tr>

</tbody></table>

<p id=""mwAas"">More a cultural than a grammatical problem is the problem of addressing somebody. Even though there exist a multitude of words with the meaning ""you"", it is generally avoided to address somebody directly. The closest equivalent to ""you"" is あなた <i id=""mwAaw"">anata</i>, but it's only used among close friends, to address people of lower status than you, or by women to address their male lovers. It is usually preferred to address somebody by family name, title or status, applying appropriate honorifics.</p>

<p id=""mwAa0"">Note that in Japan, it is generally rude to address people by first name, and last names are almost always used instead. The exception to this rule are children of elementary school age or younger, and friends you are very close to. When names are written in Japanese, they always follow the Eastern name order (like Chinese and Korean names), with the last name always written before the first name, which is contrary to common practice in English-speaking countries. This means that someone known as Taro Yamada in English will have his name written as 山田太郎 (<i id=""mwAa4"">yamada tarō</i>) in Japanese.</p>

<dl id=""mwAa8""><dt id=""mwAbA""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt35"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAbE"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""さん""}},""i"":0}}]}'>さん</span> <i id=""mwAbI"">-san</i></dt><dd id=""mwAbM"">The most basic honorific, about equivalent to Mr. or Ms. (no distinction between the two in Japanese). 山田さん <i id=""mwAbQ"">Yamada-san</i>: Mister Yamada</dd>
<dt id=""mwAbU""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt36"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAbY"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""様""}},""i"":0}}]}'>様</span> <i id=""mwAbc"">-sama</i></dt><dd id=""mwAbg"">Politer than <i id=""mwAbk"">-san</i>, used to address people ranking higher on the social ladder, such as your supervisor or boss, or even deities. It is also used by shop assistants to address customers.</dd>
<dt id=""mwAbo""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt37"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAbs"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""ちゃん""}},""i"":0}}]}'>ちゃん</span> <i id=""mwAbw"">-chan</i></dt><dd id=""mwAb0"">Used to address children of elementary school age or younger and (usually female) close friends.</dd>
<dt id=""mwAb4""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt38"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAb8"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""君""}},""i"":0}}]}'>君</span> <i id=""mwAcA"">-kun</i></dt><dd id=""mwAcE"">Used to address boys of elementary school age or younger and male close friends.</dd>
<dt id=""mwAcI""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt39"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAcM"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""先生""}},""i"":0}}]}'>先生</span> <i id=""mwAcQ"">sensei</i></dt><dd id=""mwAcU"">Used to address teachers, medical doctors and dentists. The person's surname can be appended at the beginning if necessary for disambiguation.</dd>
<dt id=""mwAcY""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt40"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAcc"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""お客様""}},""i"":0}}]}'>お客様</span> <i id=""mwAcg"">okyaku-sama</i></dt><dd id=""mwAck"">""Honored Mr/Ms customer"", used by hotel or shop employees to address you.</dd>
<dt id=""mwAco""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt41"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAcs"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""店長さん""}},""i"":0}}]}'>店長さん</span> <i id=""mwAcw"">tenchō-san</i></dt><dd id=""mwAc0"">The way to address the manager of a shop, though not the other employees.</dd>
<dt id=""mwAc4""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt42"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAc8"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""お兄さん""}},""i"":0}}]}'>お兄さん</span> <i id=""mwAdA"">onīsan</i>, <span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt43"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAdE"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""お姉さん""}},""i"":0}}]}'>お姉さん</span> <i id=""mwAdI"">onēsan</i></dt><dd id=""mwAdM"">Literally ""big brother"" and ""big sister"" respectively, is used to address young people who you're having a hard time finding a better honorific for.</dd>
<dt id=""mwAdQ""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt44"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAdU"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""お爺さん""}},""i"":0}}]}'>お爺さん</span> <i id=""mwAdY"">ojīsan</i>, <span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt45"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAdc"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""お婆さん""}},""i"":0}}]}'>お婆さん</span> <i id=""mwAdg"">obāsan</i></dt><dd id=""mwAdk"">""Grandpa"" and ""grandma"", very popular to address old people. Cuter when used with <i id=""mwAdo"">-chan</i>.</dd>
<dt id=""mwAds""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt46"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAdw"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""そちら""}},""i"":0}}]}'>そちら</span> <i id=""mwAd0"">sochira</i></dt><dd id=""mwAd4"">Means something like ""on your side"" and is used when absolutely no better honorific can be found.</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwAd8"">There are also several different words for ""I"", with 私 <i id=""mwAeA"">watashi</i> being the most commonly used. Grammatically it's often unnecessary to use the words ""you"" or ""I"" as the intended meaning is obvious from context, so they should generally be avoided. Sometimes people will also call themselves by their own name. When doing so they must not add any additional honorifics though; one only does this when addressing others.</p>

<p id=""mwAeE"">There's no specific form for ""we"" or the plural ""you"". To address groups of people you add the plural particle たち <i id=""mwAeI"">-tachi</i> to somebody within the group or the group designator.</p>

<dl id=""mwAeM""><dt id=""mwAeQ""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt47"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAeU"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""私たち""}},""i"":0}}]}'>私たち</span> <i id=""mwAeY"">watashi-tachi</i></dt><dd id=""mwAec"">lit. ""the group around myself"", meaning ""we""</dd>
<dt id=""mwAeg""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt48"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAek"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""我々""}},""i"":0}}]}'>我々</span> <i id=""mwAeo"">ware-ware</i></dt><dd id=""mwAes"">a less formal way of saying ""we""</dd>
<dt id=""mwAew""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt49"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAe0"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""あなたたち""}},""i"":0}}]}'>あなたたち</span> <i id=""mwAe4"">anata-tachi</i></dt><dd id=""mwAe8"">""the group around you"", plural ""you""</dd>
<dt id=""mwAfA""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt50"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAfE"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""子供たち""}},""i"":0}}]}'>子供たち</span> <i id=""mwAfI"">kodomo-tachi</i></dt><dd id=""mwAfM"">""a group of children"", meaning ""the children""</dd>
<dt id=""mwAfQ""><span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt51"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAfU"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""山田さんたち""}},""i"":0}}]}'>山田さんたち</span> <i id=""mwAfY"">Yamada-san-tachi</i></dt><dd id=""mwAfc"">""the group around Yamada-<i id=""mwAfg"">san</i>"", everybody you'd associate with Mr. Yamada, based on context</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwAfk"">Japanese traditionally lacked third person pronouns, and while 彼 (<i id=""mwAfo"">kare</i>) and 彼女 (<i id=""mwAfs"">kanojo</i>) were repurposed during the Meiji era as ""he"" and ""she"" respectively for translating European texts, they sound awfully stilted if you use them when talking to people. Stick to using last name + honorific instead. If you do not know the person's name, you can say この人 (<i id=""mwAfw"">ko no hito</i>, ""this person""), あの人 (<i id=""mwAf0"">a no hito</i>, ""that person"" if far from your listener) or その人 (<i id=""mwAf4"">so no hito</i>, ""that person"" if close to your listener).　</p>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id=""10"" id=""mwAf8"" aria-labelledby=""Reading_and_writing""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading2""><h2 id=""Reading_and_writing"">Reading and writing</h2><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10"" title=""Edit section: Reading and writing""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwAgA"">Reading and writing Japanese are advanced skills which take years of work to gain much real proficiency. Japanese people use three different writing systems of various complexity, two of which (<i id=""mwAgE""><b id=""mwAgI"">hiragana</b></i> and <i id=""mwAgM""><b id=""mwAgQ"">katakana</b></i>) are syllabic and relatively easy to learn with 50 characters each.</p>

<p id=""mwAgU"">The clincher is the set of Chinese characters known as <i id=""mwAgY""><b id=""mwAgc"">kanji</b></i>, roughly 2,000 of which are in daily use while many more exist. The components of kanji originated as pictures representing concepts, and even though kanji have since evolved dramatically and many have long since jettisoned any connection to the original concept, the meaning of a few simple kanji can still be guessed at (see below).</p>

<p id=""mwAgk"">One difficulty in reading Japanese lies often in the fact that a kanji can have <b id=""mwAgo"">several different pronunciations</b>. The kanji 人 for example has the meaning of ""person"", and by itself it may be pronounced <i id=""mwAgs"">hito</i>. The kanji 大 means ""big"" (imagine a person with outstretched arms) and can be pronounced as <i id=""mwAgw"">dai</i> or <i id=""mwAg0"">ō</i>. Together they form the word 大人 ""adult"" (lit. <i id=""mwAg4"">big person</i>), which is pronounced <i id=""mwAg8"">otona</i> <span typeof=""mw:Entity"" id=""mwAhA"">—</span> a pronunciation that has no relation to <i id=""mwAhE"">dai</i>, <i id=""mwAhI"">ō</i>, or <i id=""mwAhM"">hito</i>. In the word <span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt54"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAhQ"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""外国人""}},""i"":0}}]}'>外国人</span> <i id=""mwAhU"">gaikokujin</i> (""foreigner"", lit. <i id=""mwAhY"">outside country person</i>) the same kanji 人 is pronounced <i id=""mwAhc"">jin</i>. These pronunciations exist because a single kanji may be used to write one or more different words, or parts of words. These ""readings"" are normally categorized as either Sino-Japanese (<span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt55"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAhg"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""音読み""}},""i"":0}}]}'>音読み</span> <i id=""mwAhk"">on'yomi</i>, a Japanese approximation of the Chinese pronunciation of the character at the time it was introduced to Japanese) or native Japanese (<span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt56"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAho"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""訓読み""}},""i"":0}}]}'>訓読み</span> <i id=""mwAhs"">kun'yomi</i>, based on the pronunciation of a native Japanese word). Generally, kanji are read with their native Japanese reading when on their own (eg. <span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt57"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAhw"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""話""}},""i"":0}}]}'>話</span>, <i id=""mwAh0""><u id=""mwAh4"">hanashi</u></i>) and with Sino-Japanese readings when part of compound words (eg. <span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt58"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAh8"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""電話""}},""i"":0}}]}'>電話</span>, <i id=""mwAiA"">den<u id=""mwAiE"">wa</u></i>), though there are many exceptions.</p>

<p id=""mwAiI"">While knowing <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Chinese_phrasebook"" title=""Chinese phrasebook"" id=""mwAiM"">Chinese</a> will give one a huge advantage in tackling kanji, and someone who knows Chinese would generally be able to guess the meanings of new kanji with about 70% accuracy, one should still be careful. While most characters have similar meanings in both Japanese and Chinese, there are a few which have drastically different meanings. For example, the word <span lang=""hani"" about=""#mwt59"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAiQ"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""hani""},""2"":{""wt"":""手紙""}},""i"":0}}]}'>手紙</span>, literally ""hand paper"", means ""toilet paper"" (<i id=""mwAiU"">shǒuzhǐ</i>) in China, but ""letter"" (<i id=""mwAiY"">tegami</i>) in Japan; <span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt60"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAic"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""仕草""}},""i"":0}}]}'>仕草</span> (<i id=""mwAig"">shigusa</i>) 'behaviour' is only in Japanese. A list of these terms with different meanings can be referred in this <a rel=""mw:WikiLink/Interwiki"" href=""https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Appendix:False%20friends%20between%20Chinese%20and%20Japanese"" title=""wikt:Appendix:False friends between Chinese and Japanese"" class=""extiw"" id=""mwAik"">Wiktionary page</a>.</p>

<p id=""mwAio"">In addition, many kanji used in Japanese have since become less used in Chinese (e.g. <span lang=""ja"" about=""#mwt61"" typeof=""mw:Transclusion"" id=""mwAis"" data-mw='{""parts"":[{""template"":{""target"":{""wt"":""Lang"",""href"":""./Template:Lang""},""params"":{""1"":{""wt"":""ja""},""2"":{""wt"":""犬""}},""i"":0}}]}'>犬</span> <i id=""mwAiw"">inu</i>, meaning ""dog""), meaning that while a native speaker of Chinese would likely recognize them, foreign language learners of Chinese may not be familiar with these characters as they are rarely used outside of idioms, proverbs and compound words. Moreover, the difference between Chinese and Japanese syntax means that different combinations of kanji can also result in drastically different meanings with amusing results. For instance, the kanji for the Japanese surname <i id=""mwAi0"">Inukai</i> (犬飼), which means ""someone who raises dogs"" in Japanese, will be interpreted as ""to be raised by a dog"" by a Chinese speaker.</p>

<p id=""mwAi4"">Kanji are mixed with hiragana and katakana in everyday writing for historical reasons. Japan initially adopted Classical Chinese as its literary language, but later used Chinese characters as alphabets to suit Japanese grammar and syntax. Cursive forms of these characters were then simplified into hiragana by women in the imperial court, and their printed form into katakana by Buddhist monks.</p>

<p id=""mwAi8"">Until 1946, Japanese used <i id=""mwAjA"">Kyujitai</i> 'old script' is highly same to traditional Chinese (if you are not professional, you can say it is the same); but after it, Japanese uses <i id=""mwAjE"">Shinjitai</i> 'new script', which became more different from simplified Chinese. </p>

<p id=""mwAjI"">Shin.: 実</p>

<p id=""mwAjM"">Kyu. and trad. Chinese: 實</p>

<p id=""mwAjQ"">Simp. Chinese: 实</p>

<p id=""mwAjU"">Meaning: ""real""</p>

<p id=""mwAjY"">There are also several competing systems for rendering Japanese in the Latin alphabet, although the <b id=""mwAjc""><a rel=""mw:WikiLink/Interwiki"" href=""https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepburn_romanization"" title=""w:Hepburn romanization"" class=""extiw"" id=""mwAjg"">Hepburn romanization</a></b> system is the most common and is used on Wikivoyage as well.  Do not be surprised if you see these words romanized differently elsewhere.</p>

<p id=""mwAjk"">Also note that there are many <b id=""mwAjo"">homophones</b> in Japanese, i.e. words with different meanings that have the same pronunciation (like ""there"", ""they're"" and ""their""). A common trope in <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Anime_and_manga_in_Japan"" title=""Anime and manga in Japan"" id=""mwAjs"">manga</a>, this can be confusing even to native speakers, to the extent that words have to be explained with an alternative reading or need to be drawn. These words may also employ a pitch-accent system to distinguish them, which speakers of non-tonal languages may have difficulty learning to understand.</p>

<section data-mw-section-id=""11"" id=""mwAmo"" aria-labelledby=""Katakana""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Katakana"">Katakana</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11"" title=""Edit section: Katakana""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<figure typeof=""mw:File/Thumb"" id=""mwAms""><a href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/File:Kana_&amp;_Romaji_Chart.svg"" class=""mw-file-description"" id=""mwAmw""><img resource=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/File:Kana_&amp;_Romaji_Chart.svg"" src=""//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Kana_%26_Romaji_Chart.svg/500px-Kana_%26_Romaji_Chart.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikivoyage.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail"" decoding=""async"" loading=""lazy"" srcset=""//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Kana_%26_Romaji_Chart.svg/960px-Kana_%26_Romaji_Chart.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikivoyage.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x"" data-file-width=""830"" data-file-height=""537"" data-file-type=""drawing"" height=""259"" width=""400"" class=""mw-file-element"" id=""mwAm0""/></a><figcaption id=""mwAm4""><i id=""mwAm8"">Kana</i> chart</figcaption></figure>
<p id=""mwAnA"">Katakana are used to write foreign and loanwords (except for those from Chinese, which are written in kanji) and are hence a good choice for travellers to learn. The katakana set of characters encompasses exactly the same sounds as hiragana; they only look different. The table on the left only reproduces the basic character set and diacritics (カ → ガ). Combinations (キャ) apply just as for hiragana. One additional sound though is ヴ <i id=""mwAnE"">vu</i> and combinations like ヴェ <i id=""mwAnI"">ve</i> based on it, accommodating additional foreign sounds.  Every once in a while you may spot additional ingenious combinations or use of diacritics.</p>

<p id=""mwAnM"">Since Japanese doesn't very well accommodate rapid successions of consonants, the katakana transcription can often only approximate the actual pronunciation of a foreign word. While some words like café (カフェ <i id=""mwAnQ"">kafe</i>) can be represented quite gracefully, other words like beer (ビール <i id=""mwAnU"">bīru</i>) or rent-a-car (レンタカー <i id=""mwAnY"">rentakā</i>) seem slightly strange to the native English speaker. Nonetheless, many English expressions and concepts are used in everyday life, as are a number of German, French, Dutch, Portuguese and Russian loanwords. In many cases, the original words are often abbreviated when used in Japanese, such as <b id=""mwAnc"">super</b>market (スーパー <i id=""mwAng"">sūpā</i>), <b id=""mwAnk"">depart</b>ment store (デパート <i id=""mwAno"">depāto</i>), <b id=""mwAns"">remo</b>te <b id=""mwAnw"">con</b>trol (リモコン <i id=""mwAn0"">rimokon</i>) or <b id=""mwAn4"">televi</b>sion (テレビ <i id=""mwAn8"">terebi</i>). Oftentimes the exact meaning of a word has changed in Japanese (German: <i id=""mwAoA"">Arbeit</i> → アルバイト <i id=""mwAoE"">arubaito</i> is used only for part-time work, and Russian: икра <i id=""mwAoI"">ikra</i> → イクラ <i id=""mwAoM"">ikura</i> is used only for salmon roe) or a completely new meaning was invented (ワンマンカー <i id=""mwAoQ"">wanmankā</i> → ""one-man car"", trains and buses without an inspector, only <i id=""mwAoU"">one</i> driver), but you can usually at least guess at the meaning.</p>

<p id=""mwAoY"">Due to post World War II American influences, by far the largest number of non-Chinese loan words in the Japanese language come from American English, but there are also loanwords from other languages: パン from <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Portuguese_phrasebook"" title=""Portuguese phrasebook"" id=""mwAoc"">Portuguese</a> <i id=""mwAog"">pão</i> 'bread'; <b id=""mwAok"">サボ</b>る 'to sabotage' from <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/French_phrasebook"" title=""French phrasebook"" id=""mwAoo"">French</a> <i id=""mwAos"">sabotage</i> 'sabotage'.</p>

<p id=""mwAow"">To identify a katakana word, it's usually helpful to repeat it out loud a few times and to leave out superfluous vowels, especially the 'u' in ス <i id=""mwAo0"">su</i> and 'o' in ト <i id=""mwAo4"">to</i>. That way ライス <i id=""mwAo8"">raisu</i> quickly becomes ""rice"" and チケット <i id=""mwApA"">chiketto</i> becomes ""ticket"". Don't try too hard though, as sometimes original Japanese words are written in katakana as well, similar to the use of uppercase or italic letters in English. In addition, some words were not derived from English but from other languages such as German, French or Dutch.</p>

<p id=""mwApE"">Just like Japanese words from Chinese, beware of false friends written in katakana. For instance ヘルス <i id=""mwApI"">herusu</i>, from English ""health"", is actually a euphemism for prostitution that does not involve actual coitus. If you want to refer to health in the literal sense, the correct term would be 健康 <i id=""mwApM"">kenkō</i>.</p>

<div style=""font-weight:bold;"">What part of ""no"" don't you understand?</div>

<p>The Japanese are famously reluctant to say the word ""no"", and in fact the language's closest equivalent, いいえ <i>iie</i>, is largely limited to denying compliments you have received. (""Your Japanese is excellent! ""<i>Iie</i>, it is very bad!"").  But there are numerous other ways of expressing ""no"", so here are a few to watch out for.</p>

<dl><dt>いいです。<br/><i>Ii desu.</i><br/>結構です。<br/><i>Kekkō desu.</i></dt>
<dd>""It's good,"" or ""It's excellent.""  Used when you don't want more beer, don't want your <i>bentō</i> lunch microwaved, and are generally happy to keep things as they are.  Accompany with teeth-sucking and handwaving to be sure to get your point across - both of these expressions may be interpreted as <i>positive</i> responses if you don't include enough nonverbal indications to the contrary.</dd>
<dt>ちょっと難しいです･･･<br/><i>Chotto muzukashii desu...</i></dt>
<dd>Literally ""it's a little difficult"", but in practice ""it's completely impossible.""  Often just abbreviated to sucking in air through teeth, saying ""chotto"" and looking pained.  Take the hint.</dd>
<dt>申し訳ないですが･･･<br/><i>Mōshiwakenai desu ga...</i></dt>
<dd>""This is inexcusable but..."" But no. Used by sales clerks and such to tell you that you cannot do or have something.</dd>
<dt>ダメです。<br/><i>Dame desu.</i></dt>
<dd>""It's no good."" Used by equals and superiors to tell you that you cannot do or have something.  The <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Kansai"" title=""Kansai"">Kansai</a> equivalent is <i>akan</i>.</dd>
<dt>違います。<br/><i>Chigaimasu.</i></dt>
<dd>""It is different.""  What they really mean is ""you're wrong"".  The casual form <i>chigau</i> and the <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Kansai"" title=""Kansai"">Kansai</a> contraction <i>chau</i> are also much used.</dd></dl></td></tr>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id=""19"" id=""mwBDU"" aria-labelledby=""Numbers""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Numbers"">Numbers</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=19"" title=""Edit section: Numbers""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwBDY"">While Arabic (Western) numerals are employed for most uses in Japan, you will occasionally still spot Japanese numerals at eg. markets and the menus of fancy restaurants. <p>Note how many counters change form depending on the previous number: one, two, three glasses are <i>ippai</i>, <i>nihai</i>, <i>sanbai</i> respectively. There are also a few exceptions: one person and two people are <i>hitori</i> and <i>futari</i>. 20 years old is usually pronounced <i>hatachi</i>. You'll still be understood if you get these wrong though.</p>

<p>For numbers from one to nine, an old counting system is often used which applies to virtually any object you may want to count, without the need to attach a specific counter:</p>

<section data-mw-section-id=""21"" id=""mwBQM"" aria-labelledby=""Clock_time""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading4""><h4 id=""Clock_time"">Clock time</h4><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=21"" title=""Edit section: Clock time""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwBQQ"">Clock times are formed as Chinese numeral plus 時 <i id=""mwBQU"">ji</i>, for example, <i id=""mwBQY"">goji</i> 5時/五時 for five o'clock. The exception is four o'clock which is pronounced <i id=""mwBQc"">yoji</i> (四時) instead of <i id=""mwBQg"">shiji</i>. You will be understood if you simply substitute <i id=""mwBQk"">gozen</i> 午前  for ""AM"" and <i id=""mwBQo"">gogo</i> 午後 for PM, although other time qualifiers like 朝 <i id=""mwBQs"">asa</i> for morning, 昼 <i id=""mwBQw"">hiru</i> for afternoon and 夜 <i id=""mwBQ0"">yoru</i> for night sound more natural. The 24-hour clock is also commonly used in official contexts such as train schedules. TV schedules occasionally use a modified 24-hour clock, with late night showtimes counted from the previous day, e.g. Monday at 26:00 indicates <b id=""mwBQ4"">Tuesday</b> at 2:00 AM.</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""22"" id=""mwBSQ"" aria-labelledby=""Duration""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading4""><h4 id=""Duration"">Duration</h4><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=22"" title=""Edit section: Duration""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwBSU"">Confusingly, the Japanese words for ""N days"" (long) and ""Nth day"" are the same, so eg. 二日 <i id=""mwBSY"">futsuka</i> means both ""two days"" and ""the second day of the month"".  (See <a rel=""mw:WikiLink"" href=""#Days_of_the_month"" class=""mw-selflink-fragment"" id=""mwBSc"">#Days of the month</a> for the full list.)  You can tag on -間 <i id=""mwBSg"">kan</i> at the end, eg. <i id=""mwBSk"">futsukakan</i> 二日間, to clarify that you mean ""two days long"". The exception is 一日, which is read <i id=""mwBSo"">ichinichi</i> to mean ""one day/all day"", but <i id=""mwBSs"">tsuitachi</i> to mean ""first day"". Also note that 一日間 <i id=""mwBSw"">ichinichikan</i> is not used, and the term for a duration of one day is simply 一日 <i id=""mwBS0"">ichinichi</i>.</p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""28"" id=""mwBe4"" aria-labelledby=""Writing_time_and_date""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading4""><h4 id=""Writing_time_and_date"">Writing time and date</h4><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=28"" title=""Edit section: Writing time and date""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwBe8"">Dates are written in year/month/day (day of week) format, with markers:</p>

<p id=""mwBfA"">2007年3月21日(火)</p>

<p id=""mwBfE"">In Japanese, the year is read as an ordinary number with exception of ""9 as the last digit"". 1999 was ""one thousand nine hundred ninety-nine"", <i id=""mwBfI"">sen kyū-hyaku kyū-jū ku nen</i>). It can sometimes be abbreviated to the last two digits (i.e. ""ninety-nine"", <i id=""mwBfM"">kyū-jū ku nen</i>), while pronouncing it <i id=""mwBfQ"">kyū-jū <b id=""mwBfU"">kyū nen</b></i> refers to ""for the duration of 99 years"", rather the year.)</p>

<p id=""mwBfY"">Note that <b id=""mwBfc"">Imperial era years</b>, based on the name and duration of the current Emperor's reign, are also frequently used.  2020 in the Gregorian calendar corresponds to <b id=""mwBfg"">Reiwa 2</b> 令和2年, which may be abbreviated as ""R2"" or 令2.  Dates like ""02/03/24"" (Reiwa 2, March 24) are also occasionally seen. Meiji, Taishō, Shōwa, and Heisei are used by elderly people or popular on signboards at historical sights. To convert the year into Gregorian calendar:</p>
<dl id=""mwBfk""><dt id=""mwBfo"">Reiwa 令和 (1 May 2019 <span typeof=""mw:Entity"" id=""mwBfs"">–</span>)</dt><dd id=""mwBfw"">add 2018 to the year in Reiwa, i.e. <i id=""mwBf0"">Reiwa 3 nen </i> 令和3年 is 2021 in the Gregorian calendar.</dd>
<dt id=""mwBf4"">Heisei 平成 (8 January 1989 <span typeof=""mw:Entity"" id=""mwBf8"">–</span> 30 April 2019)</dt><dd id=""mwBgA"">minus 12 from the year in Heisei and add 2000, i.e. <i id=""mwBgE"">Heisei 12 nen</i> 平成12年 is 2000 in Gregorian calendar.</dd>
<dt id=""mwBgI"">Shōwa 昭和 (25 December 1926 <span typeof=""mw:Entity"" id=""mwBgM"">–</span> 7 January 1989)</dt><dd id=""mwBgQ"">plus 1925 to the year in Shōwa, i.e. <i id=""mwBgU"">Shōwa 45 nen</i> 昭和45年 is 1970 in Gregorian calendar.</dd>
<dt id=""mwBgY"">Taishō 大正 (30 July 1912 <span typeof=""mw:Entity"" id=""mwBgc"">–</span> 25 December 1926)</dt><dd id=""mwBgg"">plus 1911 to the year in Taishō, i.e. <i id=""mwBgk"">Taishō 9 nen</i> 大正9年 is 1920 in Gregorian calendar.</dd>
<dt id=""mwBgo"">Meiji 明治 (28 October 1868 <span typeof=""mw:Entity"" id=""mwBgs"">–</span> 30 July 1912)</dt><dd id=""mwBgw"">minus 33 from the year in Meiji and add 1900, i.e. <i id=""mwBg0"">Meiji 33 nen</i> 明治33年 is 1900 in Gregorian calendar.</dd></dl>

<div style=""font-weight:bold;"">Sake talk</div>

<p>Sake, known in Japanese as 日本酒 <i>nihonshu</i>, has a vocabulary all its own.  Here is a brief introduction.</p>
<dl><dt><i>atsukan</i></dt><dd>熱燗 Heated sake.  Recommended only in winter with cheap sake.</dd>
<dt><i>nurukan</i></dt><dd>ぬる燗 Heated sake in luke warm temperature below 40C. Recommended for any season with average sake.</dd>
<dt><i>hiya</i></dt><dd>冷や Sake at room temperature.</dd></dl>
<p>Note: <i>ohiya</i> お冷や means cold/iced water.</p>
<dl><dt><i>reishu</i></dt><dd>冷酒 Chilled sake.  The way to drink better sake.</dd>
<dt><i>choko</i></dt><dd>ちょこ A tiny gulp-sized ceramic cup for sake.</dd>
<dt><i>masu</i></dt><dd>升 A square wooden box traditionally used to drink chilled sake, also contains one <i>gō</i>.  Drink from the corner. Also used as a ""spiller"" to overflow your glass of <i>hiya</i> as a compliment. You can prevent to spill by sipping with the masu placed on the table.</dd>
<dt><i>tokkuri</i></dt><dd>徳利 A small ceramic jug used to pour sake, contains around one or two <i>gō/gōs</i></dd>
<dt><i>ichigō</i></dt><dd>一合 The standard measure for servings of sake, around 180 milliliters.</dd>
<dt><i>isshōbin</i></dt><dd>一升瓶 The standard sake bottle, containing 10 <i>gō</i>, ie. 1.8 liters.</dd></dl>

<div style=""font-weight:bold;"">O, honorable prefix!</div>

<p>Nearly any Japanese word can be prefixed with the respectful tags <i>o-</i> (お) or <i>go-</i> (ご or 御), often translated with the unwieldy four-syllable word ""honorable"".  A few you might expect <span typeof=""mw:Entity"">—</span> <i>o-tōsan</i> (お父さん) is ""honorable father"", and a few you might not <span typeof=""mw:Entity"">—</span> <i>o-shiri</i> (お尻) is ""honorable buttocks"". Most of the time, they're used to emphasize that the speaker is referring to the listener, so if someone enquires if after your honorable health (お元気 <i>o-genki</i>) it's proper to strip off the honorific and reply that you are merely <i>genki</i>.  However, for some words like <i>gohan</i> (ご飯) ""rice"" and <i>ocha</i> (お茶) ""tea"", the prefix is inseparable and should always be used.  In this phrasebook, the prefix is separated with a hyphen if it's optional (<i>o-kane</i>), and joined to the word if it's mandatory (<i>oisha</i>). </p></td></tr>
</tbody></table>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""44"" id=""mwCaY"" aria-labelledby=""Authority""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Authority"">Authority</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=44"" title=""Edit section: Authority""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwCac"">In Japan, you can legally be incarcerated for twenty-three (23) days before you are charged, but you do have the right to see a lawyer after the first 48 hours of detention.  Note that if you sign a confession, you <i id=""mwCag"">will</i> be convicted.</p>

<dl id=""mwCak""><dt id=""mwCao"">I haven't done anything (wrong).</dt><dd id=""mwCas"">何も(悪いことを)していません。(<i id=""mwCaw"">Nani mo (warui koto wo) shiteimasen.</i>)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCa0"">It was a misunderstanding.</dt><dd id=""mwCa4"">誤解でした。 (<i id=""mwCa8"">Gokai deshita.</i>)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCbA"">Where are you taking me?</dt><dd id=""mwCbE"">どこへ連れて行くのですか？ (<i id=""mwCbI"">Doko e tsurete yukuno desu ka?</i>)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCbM"">Am I under arrest?</dt><dd id=""mwCbQ"">私は逮捕されてるのですか？ (<i id=""mwCbU"">Watashi wa taiho sareteruno desu ka?</i>)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCbY"">I am a citizen of ____.</dt><dd id=""mwCbc"">____　の国民です。 (<i id=""mwCbg"">____ no kokumin desu.</i>)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCbk"">I want to meet with the ____ embassy.</dt><dd id=""mwCbo"">____ 大使館と会わせて下さい。 (<i id=""mwCbs"">____ taishikan to awasete kudasai.</i>)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCbw"">I want to meet with a lawyer.</dt><dd id=""mwCb0"">弁護士と会わせて下さい。(<i id=""mwCb4"">Bengoshi to awasete kudasai.</i>)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCb8"">Can it be settled with a fine?</dt><dd id=""mwCcA"">罰金で済みますか？ (<i id=""mwCcE"">Bakkin de sumimasu ka?</i>)<br id=""mwCcI""/>Note: You can say this to a traffic cop, but bribery is highly unlikely to work in Japan.</dd></dl>

<p>If words can be shortened, Japanese will inevitably shorten them. Two by two syllables is often the sweet spot, and sometimes it's hard to guess where those came from.</p>

<dt>プリクラ <i>purikura</i></dt><dd>→ プリントクラブ <i>purinto kurabu</i> or ""print club"". A sort of extremely flashy photo booth and a favourite pastime for many.</dd>

<dl id=""mwCcU""><dt id=""mwCcY"">そうですね。 <i id=""mwCcc"">Sō desu ne.</i></dt><dd id=""mwCcg"">""That's how it is, isn't it?""<br id=""mwCck""/>General agreement. Especially old people can be heard going <i id=""mwCco"">sō desu ne</i> back and forth quite a few times.</dd>

<dt id=""mwCeU"">大丈夫。 <i id=""mwCeY"">Daijōbu.</i></dt><dd id=""mwCec"">""It is alright.""<br id=""mwCeg""/>For general reassurance. Used with <i id=""mwCek"">desu ka?</i> to inquire if something or somebody is alright.</dd>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""46"" id=""mwCfs"" aria-labelledby=""Honorifics""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Honorifics"">Honorifics</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=46"" title=""Edit section: Honorifics""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwCfw"">Japanese makes extensive use of honorific language (敬語 <i id=""mwCf0"">keigo</i>) when talking to people of higher status. Keigo is famously difficult to master and even Japanese salespeople often need to take special courses to learn to speak correctly. As a foreigner, you will generally not be expected to use keigo, but it is very commonly used in situations like salespeople talking to customers and public announcements, so having at least passive familiarity with the most common <i id=""mwCf4"">keigo</i> verbs and constructs will come in very useful.</p>

<section data-mw-section-id=""47"" id=""mwCf8"" aria-labelledby=""Respectful_form""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading4""><h4 id=""Respectful_form"">Respectful form</h4><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=47"" title=""Edit section: Respectful form""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwCgA"">When talking to someone of higher status than yourself, it is important to use a respectful form (尊敬語 <i id=""mwCgE"">sonkeigo</i>) when talking about the other person. Generally, this follows the pattern お～になる(<i id=""mwCgI"">o ~ ni naru</i>), where ～ represents the stem of the basic polite form: eg. to read, 読む(<i id=""mwCgM"">yomu</i>), basic polite form 読みます(<i id=""mwCgQ"">yomimasu</i>) becomes お読みになる(<i id=""mwCgU"">o-yomi-ni-naru</i>). The <i id=""mwCgY"">naru</i> at the end follows the normal conjugation patterns for <i id=""mwCgc"">naru</i>, most commonly becoming <i id=""mwCgg"">narimasu</i> (present) or <i id=""mwCgk"">narimashita</i> (past). The main exceptions are listed below:</p>

<ul id=""mwCgo""><li id=""mwCgs"">To see: 見る becomes ご覧になる (<i id=""mwCgw"">goran-ni-naru</i>).</li>
<li id=""mwCg0"">To eat/drink: 食べる/飲む becomes 召し上がる (<i id=""mwCg4"">meshi-agaru</i>).</li>
<li id=""mwCg8"">To come/go/be at a place: 来る/行く/いる becomes いらっしゃる (<i id=""mwChA"">irassharu</i>). (basic polite form いらっしゃいます <i id=""mwChE"">irasshaimasu</i> and <b id=""mwChI"">not</b> いらっしゃります)</li>
<li id=""mwChM"">To know: 知る becomes ご存知だ (<i id=""mwChQ"">gozonji-da</i>).</li>
<li id=""mwChU"">To give (to yourself): くれる becomes 下さる (<i id=""mwChY"">kudasaru</i>). (basic polite form 下さいます <i id=""mwChc"">kudasaimasu</i> and <b id=""mwChg"">not</b> 下さります)</li>
<li id=""mwChk"">To do: する becomes なさる (<i id=""mwCho"">nasaru</i>). (basic polite form なさいます <i id=""mwChs"">nasaimasu</i> and <b id=""mwChw"">not</b> なさります)</li>
<li id=""mwCh0"">To say: 言う becomes おっしゃる (<i id=""mwCh4"">ossharu</i>) (basic polite form おっしゃいます <i id=""mwCh8"">osshaimasu</i> and <b id=""mwCiA"">not</b> おっしゃります)</li></ul>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""48"" id=""mwCiE"" aria-labelledby=""Humble_form""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading4""><h4 id=""Humble_form"">Humble form</h4><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=48"" title=""Edit section: Humble form""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwCiI"">When talking about yourself to someone of higher status than you, it is important to put yourself down by using a humble form (謙遜語 <i id=""mwCiM"">kensongo</i>). Generally this follows the pattern お～する (<i id=""mwCiQ"">o ~ suru</i>), where ～ reprents the stem of the basic polite form: eg. to borrow, 借りる(<i id=""mwCiU"">kariru</i>), basic polite form 借ります (<i id=""mwCiY"">karimasu</i>) becomes お借りする (<i id=""mwCic"">o-kari-suru</i>). The <i id=""mwCig"">suru</i> at the end follows the usual conjugation pattern of <i id=""mwCik"">suru</i>, most commonly becoming <i id=""mwCio"">shimasu</i> (present) or <i id=""mwCis"">shimashita</i> (past); for an extra helping of humility, the verb 致す <i id=""mwCiw"">itasu</i> > 致します <i id=""mwCi0"">itashimasu</i> can be substituted. The main exceptions are listed below:</p>

<ul id=""mwCi4""><li id=""mwCi8"">To see: 見る becomes 拝見する (<i id=""mwCjA"">haiken-suru</i>).</li>
<li id=""mwCjE"">To come/go: 来る/行く becomes 参る (<i id=""mwCjI"">mairu</i>).</li>
<li id=""mwCjM"">To eat/drink/receive: 食べる/飲む/もらう becomes いただく (<i id=""mwCjQ"">itadaku</i>)</li>
<li id=""mwCjU"">To give: あげる becomes さし上げる (<i id=""mwCjY"">sashi-ageru</i>).</li>
<li id=""mwCjc"">To do: する becomes 致す (<i id=""mwCjg"">itasu</i>)</li>
<li id=""mwCjk"">To know: 知る becomes 存じる (<i id=""mwCjo"">zonjiru</i>)</li>
<li id=""mwCjs"">To say: 言う becomes 申し上げる (<i id=""mwCjw"">mōshi-ageru</i>)</li>
<li id=""mwCj0"">My name is: いう becomes 申す (<i id=""mwCj4"">mōsu</i>)</li></ul>

</section><section data-mw-section-id=""49"" id=""mwCj8"" aria-labelledby=""Polite_form""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading4""><h4 id=""Polite_form"">Polite form</h4><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=49"" title=""Edit section: Polite form""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>
<p id=""mwCkA"">The third type of keigo is called simply ""polite language"", or <i id=""mwCkE"">teineigo</i> (丁寧語).  Whereas respectful and humble language refer to the <i id=""mwCkI"">subject</i> (you and I), <i id=""mwCkM"">teineigo</i> is used to simply imply respect to the <i id=""mwCkQ"">listener</i>.  An example:</p>

<dl id=""mwCkU""><dt id=""mwCkY"">りんごをご覧になりますか？ <i id=""mwCkc"">Ringo wo goran ni narimasuka?</i></dt><dd id=""mwCkg"">Can <b id=""mwCkk"">you see</b> the apple? (<i id=""mwCko"">respectful</i>)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCks"">りんごを拝見します。 <i id=""mwCkw"">Ringo wo haiken shimasu.</i></dt><dd id=""mwCk0""><b id=""mwCk4"">I see</b> the apple. (<i id=""mwCk8"">humble</i>)</dd>
<dt id=""mwClA"">彼もりんごを見ます。 <i id=""mwClE"">Kare mo ringo wo mimasu.</i></dt><dd id=""mwClI""><b id=""mwClM"">He also sees</b> the apple. (<i id=""mwClQ"">polite</i>)</dd></dl>

<p id=""mwClU"">In fact, the <i id=""mwClY"">desu</i> copula and the <i id=""mwClc"">-masu</i> form taught to beginning students of Japanese are both examples of <i id=""mwClg"">teineigo</i>.  A few verbs and adjectives have special <i id=""mwClk"">teineigo</i> forms:</p>

<dl id=""mwClo""><dt id=""mwCls"">to be</dt><dd id=""mwClw""><i id=""mwCl0"">aru</i> (ある) → <i id=""mwCl4"">gozaimasu</i> (ございます)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCl8"">to die</dt><dd id=""mwCmA""><i id=""mwCmE"">shinu</i> (死ぬ) → <i id=""mwCmI"">nakunaru</i> (亡くなる)</dd>
<dt id=""mwCmM"">good</dt><dd id=""mwCmQ""><i id=""mwCmU"">ii/yoi</i> (いい/良い) → <i id=""mwCmY"">yoroshii</i> (よろしい)</dd></dl>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id=""50"" id=""mwCmc"" aria-labelledby=""Country_and_territory_names""><div class=""mw-heading mw-heading3""><h3 id=""Country_and_territory_names"">Country and territory names</h3><span class=""mw-editsection""><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">[</span><a href=""/w/index.php?title=Japanese_phrasebook&amp;action=edit&amp;section=50"" title=""Edit section: Country and territory names""><span>edit</span></a><span class=""mw-editsection-bracket"">]</span></span></div>

<p id=""mwCtA"">For those who want proof of their proficiency in Japanese, there is the <a rel=""mw:ExtLink nofollow"" href=""https://www.jlpt.jp/e/"" class=""external text"" id=""mwCtE"">Japanese-Language Proficiency Test</a> (JLPT, 日本語能力試験), which is endorsed by the Japanese government, and serves as the Japanese-language equivalent of the TOEFL and IELTS. The test is conducted at 5 levels, with level N5 requiring only a basic level of proficiency to pass, and N1 requiring near-native proficiency. Only reading and listening is tested, and there is no oral examination. Many private Japanese-language courses are geared towards preparing students for this exam.</p>
